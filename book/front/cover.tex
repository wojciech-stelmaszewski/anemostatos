\noindent\begin{minipage}{118mm}
{\sanscaps\small\color{accent}THE NAME}\par\vspace{9mm}
{\displayserif\fontsize{34}{40}\selectfont ἀνεμόστατος}\par\vspace{3mm}
{\displayserif\itshape\fontsize{14}{18}\selectfont anemóstatos — “the one that stands in the wind”}\par\vspace{12mm}
\rmfamily\linespread{1.16}\selectfont
{\displayserif\fontsize{13}{16}\selectfont ἄνεμος} \emph{(ánemos)}, \emph{wind}. Homer's word for every wind there is,
from Boreas in the north to Zephyros in the west. Its root, \emph{*h₂enh₁-}, “to breathe”, also gave Latin
\emph{animus} and \emph{anima}, breath and soul: for the Greeks, wind and breath were one thing seen at two sizes.\par\medskip
{\displayserif\fontsize{13}{16}\selectfont στατός} \emph{(statós)}, \emph{standing, set in place}. The verbal adjective of
\emph{ἵστημι}, “to make stand”, from the root \emph{*steh₂-} that runs through Latin \emph{stare} and \emph{status},
English \emph{stand} and \emph{static}, Polish \emph{stać}. Homer uses it of a horse kept standing in its stall,
\emph{στατὸς ἵππος} (\emph{Iliad} 6.506).\par\medskip
Greek joins the two with a linking \emph{-o-} and pulls the accent as far back as it will go, as in \emph{θεόδοτος},
“god-given”. So a Greek would say \emph{anemóstatos}; English drops the accent, as it does in every word it
borrows from Greek. As far as we know no ancient author used the word: it is made here, by the old rules.
With \emph{statós} in its Homeric sense, it names the one that stands still in the wind.\par\medskip
The same \emph{-stat} lives on in the workshop: the \emph{thermostat} holds a room at its temperature, the
\emph{aerostat} (a balloon) stands in the air, the \emph{rheostat} holds back a current. Each is a device that holds
something still, which is a fair one-word definition of a controller. An \emph{anemostatos} holds \emph{itself}
still against the wind: the quadrotor of this book, hovering through the gusts. (In Polish, \emph{anemostat}
already names a humbler thing, the round grille in an office ceiling that spreads the air. Same roots,
opposite job: it makes the draught; ours stands in it.)
