A drone hovers two metres above the floor. Gravity pulls it down with a force of almost ten newtons, and the wind shoves it around at random. Nobody tells the drone how strong the next gust will be. And yet it stays where it was told to stay. This first lesson is about the one idea that makes that possible, taken apart slowly, piece by piece.}

\section{The problem}

Anemostatos begins with the simplest vehicle it can offer: a quadrotor that can only move \emph{up and down}. Its four motors always push together, so the only thing that can be chosen is their total thrust $T$. Before we can control anything we need to know how this vehicle moves, so let us write down its equation of motion carefully.

\subsection{The forces}
Three kinds of force act on the drone along the vertical axis (\cref{fig:meet-fbd}):
\marginnote{\resizebox{\marginparwidth}{!}{\input{tikz/freebody}}\par\vspace{3pt}\captionsetup{type=figure}\caption{The forces on the L1 drone. We count forces and positions as positive upwards.}\label{fig:meet-fbd}}[-88pt]
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{thrust} $T$ of the four propellers, upwards, which we choose;
  \item the \textbf{weight} $mg$, downwards, with $m$ the mass and $g = \SI{9.81}{m/s^2}$;
  \item everything else, which we collect in one term $d(t)$ and call the \textbf{disturbance}: the drag of the air flowing past the drone, the push of the wind, a hand poking it. It can point up or down, and we do not know it in advance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The equation of motion}
Newton's second law says that the mass times the acceleration equals the sum of the forces. With the altitude $y$ measured upwards from the floor, the acceleration is $\ddot y$ (two dots: the second derivative with respect to time), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-plant}
  m\,\ddot y = T - m g + d(t).
\end{equation}
This single line is the \term{model} of our plant for the whole first half of Part~I. It is worth reading it slowly. If the thrust exactly equals the weight and there is no disturbance, the right-hand side is zero, the acceleration is zero, and the drone keeps whatever vertical velocity it has — at rest, it stays at rest. Any imbalance of forces produces an acceleration, and an acceleration, however small, changes first the velocity and then the position.\aside{The full physics model of the simulator — motor lag, quadratic drag, the gust generator — is in the \hyperref[ch:prelude]{Prelude}. Here Equation~\eqref{eq:meet-plant} is all we need.}

The numbers of the simulated drone: $m = \SI{1}{kg}$, so its weight is \SI{9.81}{N}; the four motors can deliver at most $4 \times \SI{6.1}{N} = \SI{24.4}{N}$ together.\innumbers{Ten newtons is the weight of a one-litre carton of milk. Hold one at arm's length: that is what the four motors do, all the time.}

\subsection{The goal}
Our task is to choose the thrust $T(t)$ at every moment so that the altitude $y(t)$ stays at a desired value $r$, called the \term{setpoint}, even though the disturbance $d(t)$ is unknown. In this lesson $r = \SI{2}{m}$.

\section{First attempt: compute the answer}

In still air the answer looks obvious. Hovering means no acceleration, $\ddot y = 0$, and with $d = 0$ Equation~\eqref{eq:meet-plant} gives $T = mg$. So we set the thrust to \SI{9.81}{N} and are done.

This strategy is called \term{open-loop} control: the command is computed from a model, once, and never checked against what actually happens. It works exactly as well as the model.\didyouknow{Open loop is not always wrong. A washing machine, a traffic light on a timer and a thrown ball are open-loop: cheap, and good enough when nothing unexpected happens.} Let us test it against a very small imperfection.

\begin{example}[An open-loop hover with a slightly wrong mass]
\label{ex:meet-open}
The battery is \SI{50}{g} heavier than we thought: the true mass is $m = \SI{1.05}{kg}$, but we compute the thrust with $\hat m = \SI{1.00}{kg}$. (A hat on a symbol means \enquote{our estimate of} it.) How far has the drone sunk after one second, and when does it hit the floor?

\textbf{Step 1 — the commanded thrust.} $T = \hat m g = 1.00 \cdot 9.81 = \SI{9.81}{N}$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the true weight.} $mg = 1.05 \cdot 9.81 = \SI{10.30}{N}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the net force.} With $d = 0$, $T - mg = 9.81 - 10.30 = \SI{-0.49}{N}$: a small, constant downward force.

\textbf{Step 4 — the acceleration.} \Cref{eq:meet-plant} gives $\ddot y = -0.49/1.05 = \SI{-0.467}{m/s^2}$, and it is constant, because the force is constant.

\textbf{Step 5 — integrate once.} A constant acceleration $a$ changes the velocity linearly: starting from rest, $\dot y(t) = a\,t = -0.467\,t$.

\textbf{Step 6 — integrate again.} The position changes quadratically: $y(t) = y_0 + \tfrac12 a t^2 = y_0 - 0.233\,t^2$.\recall{Constant acceleration $a$ from rest: $v = at$ and $s = \tfrac12 at^2$. Two integrations of a constant.}

\textbf{Step 7 — evaluate.} After \SI{1}{s}: $0.233 \cdot 1^2 = \SI{0.23}{m}$ lower. The floor is \SI{2}{m} below, reached when $0.233\,t^2 = 2$, i.e.\ after $t = \sqrt{2/0.233} \approx \SI{2.9}{s}$.
\end{example}

The example shows something important about this plant. A force error does not produce a position error of the same size; it is \emph{integrated twice} on its way to the position — once into velocity, once into position. Such a plant is called a \term{double integrator}, and it has no tendency to return anywhere: every constant force error ends with the drone on the floor or in the sky. The wind supplies force errors all the time.\didyouknow{Ice is a double integrator. A hockey puck keeps the velocity it was given and has no favourite place. The drone in still air is a puck on vertical ice.}

\section{Second attempt: look and correct}

A human pilot would not fly like that. A pilot looks at the drone, sees that it is sinking, and gives more throttle; sees it rising too high, and gives less. This is the idea of \term{feedback}, and it is as old as steering a boat.

\begin{keyidea}[Feedback]
Instead of predicting the right command, \emph{measure} what actually happens, \emph{compare} it with what should happen, and \emph{act} on the difference. Repeat forever. A feedback controller does not need to know the disturbance to reject it: it only needs to see its effect.
\end{keyidea}

Everyday life is full of such loops.\tip{Look for loops around you and name their parts: what is measured, what is it compared with, what acts? It is the fastest way to learn the vocabulary.} When you adjust a shower, you feel the water, compare with the temperature you like, and turn the tap — and if the pipes are long, you know the typical failure too: the water reacts late, you overcorrect, and it swings from too hot to too cold. That failure has a name and a lesson of its own (\lessonref{les:slow}). When you drive in a lane, you watch the distance to the edge and steer to keep it. In both cases you do not model the plumbing or the car; you react to what you see.

\subsection{The loop and its vocabulary}

\Cref{fig:meet-loop} is the picture behind every chapter of this book. Its pieces have standard names, and it is worth learning them now, because the simulator uses them everywhere — with the same colours as in this book.

\sidecapfig{\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{tikz/loop}}}{The feedback loop. The error (red) is the only thing the controller reacts to; the measurement (blue) is the only thing it knows about the drone. The wind enters the plant, not the controller.}{fig:meet-loop}

\begin{definition}{The parts of a control loop}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=3pt, font=\sffamily\small\bfseries]
  \item[Plant] the system being controlled: here the drone, described by Equation~\eqref{eq:meet-plant}.
  \item[Setpoint $r$] the desired value of the controlled variable (also called the \emph{reference}).
  \item[Measurement $y$] what the sensor reports. It may differ from the truth by noise, bias or delay; in this lesson the sensor is perfect.
  \item[Error $e = r - y$] how far the measurement is from the setpoint. Positive $e$ means \enquote{too low}.
  \item[Command $u$] what the controller asks of the actuators; here the thrust command.
  \item[Actuator] the part that turns a command into a physical effect: the motors.
  \item[Disturbance $d$] every input that we do not choose and usually do not measure.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

Notice where the arrows go. The wind enters the \emph{plant}. The controller only sees the error. This is not a limitation, it is the point: whatever the disturbance is — a gust, a wrong mass, a tired battery — it can only hurt us by creating an error, and the error is exactly what the controller watches.\didyouknow{Norbert Wiener named the science of feedback \emph{cybernetics} (1948), from the Greek \emph{kybernētēs}, the steersman. The Latin form of the same word gave English \emph{governor}.}

\subsection{The simplest feedback law, and why it already works}
Before building the full controller, let us check that even the crudest feedback beats the open loop. Take the open-loop thrust and add a correction proportional to the error:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-p}
  T = \hat m g + K_p\,e = \hat m g + K_p\,(r - y),
\end{equation}
where $K_p$ is a positive number, the \term{proportional gain}, in newtons per metre. If the drone is \SI{10}{cm} too low and $K_p = 10$, the controller adds \SI{1}{N} of thrust.\innumbers{\SI{10}{N/m} is a soft spring: a \SI{1}{kg} mass hanging on it would stretch it by almost a metre.}

\begin{example}[The same wrong mass, now with feedback]
\label{ex:meet-closed}
Same drone as in Example~\ref{ex:meet-open} ($m = 1.05$, $\hat m = 1.00$), now with the law \eqref{eq:meet-p} and $K_p = 10$. Where can the drone come to rest?

\textbf{Step 1 — insert the law into the plant.} With $d = 0$:
$m\ddot y = \hat m g + K_p(r - y) - mg$.

\textbf{Step 2 — look for a resting point.} If the drone comes to rest at some altitude $y^*$, then $\ddot y = 0$ there, and the equation becomes $0 = \hat m g + K_p(r - y^*) - mg$.\tip{At rest, set every derivative to zero and solve what is left. An equilibrium costs one line of algebra.}

\textbf{Step 3 — solve for the error at rest.} $K_p(r - y^*) = (m - \hat m)g$, so
\begin{equation*}
  e^* = r - y^* = \frac{(m - \hat m)\,g}{K_p} = \frac{0.05 \cdot 9.81}{10} = \SI{0.049}{m}.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 4 — compare.} The open loop lost the drone in three seconds. With feedback it can rest \SI{4.9}{cm} below the setpoint, indefinitely. The missing \SI{0.49}{N} is supplied by the correction term: \SI{4.9}{cm} of error times \SI{10}{N/m}.

\textbf{Step 5 — what we have not checked.} Whether the drone actually \emph{reaches} that resting point, and how. That is a question about motion, not about rest, and it is the subject of \lessonref{les:spring}. (Spoiler: this law alone makes the drone bounce around the resting point forever. We will need more than one term.)
\end{example}

\section{The PID law}

What should the controller do with the error? Anemostatos uses, in Part~I, the most successful answer in the history of engineering, the \term{PID} controller. It adds three corrections, each looking at the error from a different point in time, to the open-loop guess:\pitfall{The order of the letters is not the order of importance. Many industrial loops are PI. Ours is built as P, then PD, then PID.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-pid}
  u(t) \;=\; \underbrace{K_p\,e(t)}_{\text{\Pt}} \;+\; \underbrace{K_i\!\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\dd\tau}_{\text{\It}} \;+\; \underbrace{K_d\,\dot e(t)}_{\text{\Dt}} \;+\; \underbrace{\hat m\,g}_{\text{\FFt}} .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}{The four parts of the PID law}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=3pt, font=\sffamily\small\bfseries]
  \item[\Pt — proportional.] $K_p\,e$: proportional to the \emph{present} error. The larger the error, the harder the push. Unit of $K_p$: \si{N/m}.
  \item[\It — integral.] $K_i\int_0^t e\,\dd\tau$: proportional to the error \emph{accumulated} since the start — the area under its curve. A small error that persists eventually produces a large push. Unit of $K_i$: \si{N/(m.s)}.
  \item[\Dt — derivative.] $K_d\,\dot e$: proportional to the \emph{rate of change} of the error — where the error is going. It pushes against fast changes. Unit of $K_d$: \si{N.s/m}.
  \item[\FFt — feedforward.] $\hat m g$: not feedback at all, but the open-loop guess of the weight, with $\hat m$ the mass the controller \emph{believes} in. Feedback then only has to correct the guess.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

The units are worth checking once, because they show that each gain converts its own kind of information into a force: metres into newtons, metre-seconds into newtons, metres per second into newtons.\tip{Check the units of every new formula. If a gain times its signal is not a force, something is wrong — and you know it before computing a single number.} The default tune of the altitude loop is
\begin{equation}
  K_p = \SI{10}{N/m},\qquad K_i = \SI{0.8}{N/(m.s)},\qquad K_d = \SI{7}{N.s/m}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[What each term says, in newtons]
\label{ex:meet-terms}
The drone is \SI{10}{cm} below the setpoint, has been about that low for the last \SI{2}{s}, and is now rising at \SI{0.5}{m/s}. What does each term contribute?

\textbf{P.} $e = +0.10$\,m, so $K_p e = 10 \cdot 0.10 = +\SI{1.0}{N}$: push up, because we are too low.

\textbf{I.} The error has been about \SI{0.10}{m} for \SI{2}{s}, so its integral is about $0.10 \cdot 2 = \SI{0.2}{m.s}$, and $K_i \cdot 0.2 = 0.8 \cdot 0.2 = +\SI{0.16}{N}$: a small extra push that keeps growing as long as the error lasts.

\textbf{D.} The setpoint is constant and the drone rises at \SI{0.5}{m/s}, so the error is \emph{shrinking} at \SI{0.5}{m/s}: $\dot e = \dot r - \dot y = 0 - 0.5 = \SI{-0.5}{m/s}$. Then $K_d\dot e = 7 \cdot (-0.5) = -\SI{3.5}{N}$: push \emph{down}, although we are still too low!\pitfall{A positive error that is \emph{shrinking} has $\dot e < 0$. The D term pushes against the motion, not against the error.}

\textbf{FF.} $\hat m g = +\SI{9.81}{N}$.

\textbf{Total.} $u = 1.0 + 0.16 - 3.5 + 9.81 = \SI{7.47}{N}$, less than the weight: the drone is decelerating. The D term has seen that at \SI{0.5}{m/s} the drone would overshoot the setpoint, and brakes early. This anticipation is the essence of the derivative term, and \lessonref{les:damper} is devoted to it.
\end{example}

\subsection{A controller that runs in steps}
Equation~\eqref{eq:meet-pid} is written in continuous time, but a flight computer does not think continuously. Anemostatos runs the altitude controller at \SI{250}{Hz}: every $\Delta t = \SI{4}{ms}$ it reads the sensor, computes $u$, and holds that command until the next tick.\aside{The physics itself runs at \SI{1}{kHz}. The controller sees only every fourth step, exactly like a real autopilot that samples a continuous world.} Let $t_k = k\,\Delta t$ be the sampling instants and $e_k = e(t_k)$ the sampled errors. Two approximations turn the continuous law into a recipe the computer can follow.

\paragraph{The integral becomes a running sum.} The integral is the area under the error curve. Between two samples the error hardly changes, so the area of that thin strip is about its height times its width, $e_k\,\Delta t$ — a rectangle. Adding the rectangles one by one gives the running sum
\begin{equation}
  I_k = I_{k-1} + K_i\,e_k\,\Delta t .
\end{equation}
This is the Riemann sum from calculus, and it approaches the integral as $\Delta t \to 0$.\recall{Riemann sum: $\int_a^b f\,\dd t \approx \sum_k f(t_k)\,\Delta t$. The rectangle rule is the crudest of them, and good enough here.}

\paragraph{The derivative becomes a difference quotient.} The derivative is the slope of the error curve. The slope of the chord between the last two samples approximates it:
\begin{equation}
  \dot e(t_k) \approx \frac{e_k - e_{k-1}}{\Delta t}.
\end{equation}
By Taylor's theorem, $e_{k-1} = e_k - \dot e\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\ddot e\,\Delta t^2 - \dots$, so the error of this approximation is about $\tfrac12\ddot e\,\Delta t$: it vanishes as $\Delta t \to 0$.\pitfall{Dividing by $\Delta t = 0.004$ multiplies by 250. Any error in the two samples is multiplied too. \lessonref{les:noise} pays this bill.}

Together:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-discrete}
\begin{aligned}
  e_k &= r_k - y_k, & I_k &= I_{k-1} + K_i\,e_k\,\Delta t,\\
  D_k &= K_d\,\frac{e_k - e_{k-1}}{\Delta t}, & u_k &= K_p e_k + I_k + D_k + \hat m g .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Why is \SI{4}{ms} small enough? Because the drone moves on a time scale of about a second (we will compute it in \lessonref{les:spring}): between two samples it barely moves, and the rectangles and chords are excellent approximations.\innumbers{Real multicopters run their fastest loops at 1 to \SI{8}{kHz}. An airliner's autopilot is content with a few tens of hertz: a big aircraft moves slowly.} Most of Part~I is about what these four lines nevertheless get wrong in practice — saturation, kicks, noise, delay — and how to fix it.

\begin{example}[Three ticks of the controller]
\label{ex:meet-ticks}
The drone hovers at the setpoint with $I = 0$ when a gust pushes it down. The measured errors at four consecutive ticks are $e_{-1} = 0.016$, $e_0 = 0.020$, $e_1 = 0.024$, $e_2 = 0.026$ (in metres). Compute $u_0, u_1, u_2$ with the default gains and $\Delta t = \SI{0.004}{s}$.

\textbf{Tick 0.} $P = 10 \cdot 0.020 = 0.200$. $I_0 = 0 + 0.8 \cdot 0.020 \cdot 0.004 = 0.000064$. $D = 7 \cdot (0.020 - 0.016)/0.004 = 7 \cdot 1.0 = 7.000$. $u_0 = 0.200 + 0.000 + 7.000 + 9.81 = \SI{17.01}{N}$.

\textbf{Tick 1.} $P = 0.240$. $I_1 = 0.000064 + 0.8\cdot0.024\cdot0.004 = 0.000141$. $D = 7 \cdot 0.004/0.004 = 7.000$. $u_1 = \SI{17.05}{N}$.

\textbf{Tick 2.} $P = 0.260$. $I_2 = 0.000141 + 0.000083 = 0.000224$. $D = 7 \cdot 0.002/0.004 = 3.500$. So $u_2 = \SI{13.57}{N}$.

\textbf{Reading the numbers.} D dominates: the drone is sinking at \SIrange{0.5}{1}{m/s} and D answers with several newtons, while P adds a few tenths and I, over a few milliseconds, almost nothing. As the sinking slows (the error changes less between ticks), D drops back at once. Each term acts on its own time scale: D within milliseconds, P over tenths of a second, I over seconds.
\end{example}

\section{Watching it work}

\Cref{fig:meet-overview} shows the first forty seconds of the lesson's run, drawn from the simulator's own telemetry. The drone takes off, climbs to its two-metre setpoint and then spends the rest of the time being pushed around by turbulence.

\simfig{meet_overview}{Lesson I.1 in numbers. At $t \approx \SI{16}{s}$ a gust blows upward at \SI{4}{m/s} and lifts the drone \SI{22}{cm}. The error goes negative, P (orange) and D (violet) push down together, and in two seconds it is over. I (green) hardly moves: it is built for slow, persistent errors, not for quick ones.}{fig:meet-overview}

Look at the gust at $t \approx \SI{16}{s}$ in detail, because it contains the whole lesson:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The upward wind (bottom panel) reaches \SI{4}{m/s}. Air rushing up past the drone drags it upwards with a force of up to $c_v w^2 = 0.25 \cdot 4^2 = \SI{4}{N}$. The controller cannot see the wind.\innumbers{\SI{4}{N} is 40\,\% of the drone's weight. Yet \SI{4}{m/s} is only a gentle breeze, force 3 on the Beaufort scale.}
  \item It does see the altitude rise above the setpoint, so the error becomes negative. \Pt turns that into a downward push: at the peak, $K_p e = 10 \cdot (-0.22) = \SI{-2.2}{N}$.
  \item \Dt reacts even earlier. It sees the \emph{velocity} of the rise and pushes down while the error is still small — then, as the drone comes back down, it reverses and brakes the descent so that it does not overshoot.
  \item Once the gust is over, both terms return to zero, and the thrust returns to the feedforward value $\hat m g = \SI{9.81}{N}$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Testing the two predictions}

\marginfig{meet_off}{Switch the controller off (\key{M}) and the drone falls: nothing holds it up anymore.}{fig:meet-off}%
Examples~\ref{ex:meet-open} and~\ref{ex:meet-closed} each made a prediction about a drone that is \SI{50}{g} heavier than its controller believes: without feedback it is on the floor in about three seconds; with a proportional correction it rests \SI{4.9}{cm} low. A prediction is worth as much as its test, so we fly both. For the first ten seconds the full PID holds the heavy drone at \SI{2}{m}; its integral has quietly found the missing \SI{0.49}{N}. Then we take the feedback away.

\simfig{meet_predict}{The two predictions of this lesson against the simulator. Left: all three terms are switched off and only the \SI{9.81}{N} feedforward remains. Right: only the integral is switched off, so P and D must carry the unknown \SI{0.49}{N}.}{fig:meet-predict}

\paragraph{With feedback (right).} The drone sinks and comes to rest at \SI{1.951}{m}. Its error, \SI{0.0490}{m}, is the number of Example~\ref{ex:meet-closed} to the last digit. Equilibria are easy to predict, because at rest nothing moves and everything that depends on motion drops out of the equation.

\paragraph{Without feedback (left).} The body of the drone rests on its landing gear at \SI{0.052}{m}, so the fall is \SI{1.948}{m} and the model of Example~\ref{ex:meet-open} predicts touchdown after $\sqrt{2 \cdot 1.948/0.467} = \SI{2.89}{s}$. For the first second the dashed curve and the simulator agree. Then the simulator falls behind, and it lands after \SI{3.15}{s}. Our model has left something out, and the shape of the disagreement says what: the error grows with the speed. It is the air.

\begin{example}[The fall, with drag]
\label{ex:meet-drag}
The simulator's air resists the motion with a force $c_v v^2$, where $c_v = \SI{0.25}{N.s^2/m^2}$. Repeat Example~\ref{ex:meet-open} with it.

\textbf{Step 1 — the equation.} Let $u$ be the speed \emph{downwards}. The net downward force without drag is $F = (m - \hat m)g = \SI{0.4905}{N}$, and the drag pushes up:
\begin{equation*}
  m\,\dot u = F - c_v u^2 .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 2 — the speed limit.} The acceleration vanishes when drag equals $F$, at the \term{terminal velocity}
\begin{equation*}
  u_T = \sqrt{F/c_v} = \sqrt{0.4905/0.25} = \SI{1.40}{m/s}.
\end{equation*}
The drone can never fall faster.\innumbers{Terminal velocities: a large raindrop \SI{9}{m/s}, a skydiver lying flat \SI{55}{m/s}. Our drone, pulled by only \SI{0.49}{N}: \SI{1.4}{m/s}.} The drag-free model predicted $0.467 \cdot 2.89 = \SI{1.35}{m/s}$ at touchdown, almost the limit. That comparison alone shows that drag cannot be neglected here.

\textbf{Step 3 — solve for the speed.} With $a = F/m = \SI{0.467}{m/s^2}$ the equation reads $\dot u = a\,(1 - u^2/u_T^2)$. Separate the variables and integrate from rest:
\begin{gather*}
  \int_0^u \frac{\dd u'}{1 - u'^2/u_T^2} = a\,t
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  u_T \operatorname{artanh}\frac{u}{u_T} = a\,t ,\\
  u(t) = u_T \tanh\frac{a\,t}{u_T}.
\end{gather*}
For small $t$, $\tanh z \approx z$ and $u \approx a\,t$: the old answer. For large $t$, $\tanh z \to 1$ and $u \to u_T$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the distance.} Since $\int\tanh z\,\dd z = \ln\cosh z$, the distance fallen is\recall{$\cosh z = \tfrac12(e^z + e^{-z})$ and $\tanh z = \sinh z/\cosh z$. The inverse used in Step~5: $\operatorname{arcosh} c = \ln\!\big(c + \sqrt{c^2 - 1}\big)$.}
\begin{equation*}
  s(t) = \frac{u_T^2}{a}\,\ln\cosh\frac{a\,t}{u_T}, \qquad \frac{u_T^2}{a} = \frac{1.962}{0.467} = \SI{4.20}{m}.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 5 — touchdown.} Set $s = 1.948$: $\ln\cosh z = 1.948/4.20 = 0.464$, so $\cosh z = 1.590$ and $z = \ln\!\big(1.590 + \sqrt{1.590^2 - 1}\big) = 1.039$. Then $t = z\,u_T/a = 1.039 \cdot 1.40/0.467 = \SI{3.12}{s}$, at a speed of $1.40\tanh 1.039 = \SI{1.09}{m/s}$.

\textbf{Step 6 — compare.} The simulator lands after \SI{3.15}{s} at \SI{1.09}{m/s}. The dotted curve in \cref{fig:meet-predict} lies almost on the measurement.

\textbf{Step 7 — the last \SI{0.03}{s}.} The motors do not drop from \SI{10.30}{N} to \SI{9.81}{N} at once; they follow with a time constant of \SI{30}{ms}. For that long the drone still has its extra \SI{0.49}{N}, an impulse of $0.49 \cdot 0.03 = \SI{0.015}{N.s}$, which leaves it \SI{1.4}{cm/s} slower than the model for the whole fall: about \SI{4}{cm} after three seconds, which takes three to four hundredths of a second to cover at \SI{1.09}{m/s}.
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}[A model is a choice]
Three models of the same fall: without drag, with drag, with drag and motor lag. Each is more accurate and more work than the one before. None is \enquote{the} model. The right one is the simplest whose error does not change the decision — and for designing a feedback loop the first is usually enough, because feedback itself corrects what the model leaves out. That is why \cref{eq:meet-plant} carries us through half of Part~I. When a prediction and a flight disagree, the disagreement is information: ask what grows the way the error grows.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The engineer's view: state, and the limits of feedback}

Every lesson of this book ends its theory with a short section like this one. It says the same thing again in the language that engineers use at work, and it is where the threads to the later parts of the course begin.

\subsection{The state}
To predict where the drone will be a moment from now, it is not enough to know where it is. We also need its velocity. With those two numbers and the forces, Newton's law gives everything that follows. Nothing else about the past matters.\didyouknow{Laplace's famous demon — know every position and velocity now, and the whole future follows — is the definition of state, applied to the universe.}

\begin{definition}{State}
The \term{state} of a dynamical system is the smallest set of numbers that, together with the inputs from now on, determines its future. For the L1 drone it is the altitude and the vertical velocity, $(y, v)$.
\end{definition}

Written with the state, the second-order equation \eqref{eq:meet-plant} becomes two first-order ones:
\begin{equation*}
  \dot y = v, \qquad \dot v = \frac{1}{m}\,\big(T - m g + d\big),
\end{equation*}
or, collected in one line of matrices,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-state}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix} y \\ v \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} y \\ v \end{pmatrix}
  + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1/m \end{pmatrix}\big(T - m g + d\big).
\end{equation}
The matrix form looks like bureaucracy for so small a system. Its value appears when the vehicle has twelve state variables instead of two, and the same notation still fits on one line.\innumbers{A quadrotor as a rigid body has twelve state variables: three each for position, velocity, attitude and angular velocity.} As a picture, the plant is a force divided by the mass and integrated twice:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
  \node[lab] (f) {$T - mg + d$};
  \node[blk, right=7mm of f] (m) {$1/m$};
  \node[blk, right=12mm of m] (i1) {$\int$};
  \node[blk, right=12mm of i1] (i2) {$\int$};
  \coordinate[right=10mm of i2] (o);
  \draw[sig] (f) -- (m);
  \draw[sig] (m) -- node[above, lab] {$\dot v$} (i1);
  \draw[sig] (i1) -- node[above, lab] {$v$} (i2);
  \draw[sig] (i2) -- node[above, lab] {$y$} (o);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
This is the \emph{double integrator} of Example~\ref{ex:meet-open}, and it is literally how the simulator computes. One thousand times per second, \src{src/sim/dynamics.ts} adds the acceleration times one millisecond to the velocity, and then the new velocity times one millisecond to the position: the two integrals of the picture, as two lines of code.

\subsection{What feedback cannot do}
Feedback is powerful, and it is worth knowing its limits on the first day.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{It acts after the fact.} A feedback controller does nothing until an error exists. If you know what is coming — the weight, a planned manoeuvre — acting on that knowledge in advance is better. That is feedforward (\lessonref{les:droop}).\tip{Good designs use both: feedforward for everything you know, feedback for everything you do not.}
  \item \textbf{It can make things worse.} A loop that corrects too hard or too late oscillates or diverges. The next lesson meets the first case and \lessonref{les:edge} finds the limit.
  \item \textbf{It trusts its sensor.} This one deserves a box.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{watchout}[Feedback is only as good as its sensor]
The loop drives the \emph{measurement} to the setpoint, not the truth. If the altimeter reads $b$ too high, the controller is satisfied when $y + b = r$, so the drone flies at $y = r - b$: exactly $b$ too low, however good the gains, integral or not. Feedback removes the effect of every disturbance that enters \emph{after} the comparison and of none that enters through the sensor. This is why the second half of Part~I is about sensors, and why estimation takes as many lessons of this course as control does.
\end{watchout}

\subsection{In formal terms}
The statements of this lesson, made exact. Every lesson has such a subsection. It can be skipped on a first reading, and should not be on the second.

\begin{definition}{Linear time-invariant system}
A system is \term{linear and time-invariant} (LTI) if its state $\vx(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ obeys
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-lti}
  \dot{\vx} = A\,\vx + B\,\symbf u, \qquad \symbf y = C\,\vx ,
\end{equation}
with constant matrices $A$ ($n \times n$), $B$ ($n \times p$) and $C$ ($q \times n$), an input $\symbf u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and an output $\symbf y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^q$. For the L1 drone of \cref{eq:meet-state}, $n = 2$ and $\vx = (y, v)^\T$; the input is the net force $u = T - mg + d$, the output is the altitude, and
\begin{equation*}
  A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
  B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1/m \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
  C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The solution of a linear system]
\label{thm:meet-solution}
Let $A$ and $B$ be constant real matrices and $\symbf u$ a piecewise continuous function on $[0, \infty)$. Then for every initial state $\vx_0$ the problem $\dot{\vx} = A\vx + B\symbf u$, $\vx(0) = \vx_0$, has exactly one solution,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:meet-voc}
  \vx(t) = e^{At}\,\vx_0 + \int_0^t e^{A(t - \tau)}\,B\,\symbf u(\tau)\,\dd\tau ,
  \qquad\text{where}\qquad e^{At} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(At)^k}{k!} .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The series converges for every $t$, because $\|(At)^k/k!\| \le (\|A\|\,t)^k/k!$, and it may be differentiated term by term, which gives $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}e^{At} = A\,e^{At}$. Differentiating \cref{eq:meet-voc} then gives $\dot{\vx} = A\vx + B\symbf u$ wherever $\symbf u$ is continuous, and $\vx(0) = \vx_0$. For uniqueness let $\symbf z$ be the difference of two solutions. Then $\dot{\symbf z} = A\symbf z$ and $\symbf z(0) = 0$, so $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(e^{-At}\symbf z\big) = e^{-At}(\dot{\symbf z} - A\symbf z) = 0$. Hence $e^{-At}\symbf z$ keeps its initial value, zero, and since $e^{-At}$ has the inverse $e^{At}$, $\symbf z = 0$.
\end{proof}

This theorem is what makes the state a state:\recall{The scalar case, from a first course in differential equations: $\dot x = ax + bu$ has the solution $x = e^{at}x_0 + \int_0^t e^{a(t-\tau)}\,b\,u\,\dd\tau$. The theorem is the same formula with matrices.} $\vx_0$ and the input from now on fix the whole future, and nothing else is needed. For the drone the series even stops after two terms, because $A^2 = 0$:
\begin{equation*}
  e^{At} = \Id + At = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
and for a constant net force $u_0$ \cref{eq:meet-voc} becomes
\begin{equation*}
  y(t) = y_0 + v_0 t + \frac{u_0}{2m}\,t^2, \qquad v(t) = v_0 + \frac{u_0}{m}\,t .
\end{equation*}
Example~\ref{ex:meet-open} is this formula with $u_0 = \SI{-0.49}{N}$. The model with drag is not linear and has no such formula, but it still has exactly one solution from every initial state, because its right-hand side is continuously differentiable (Khalil, 2002, ch.~3).

\begin{theorem}[A loop with integral action regulates its measurement]
\label{thm:meet-sensor}
Let a controller contain a state $I$ with $\dot I = K_i\,(r - y_m)$, where $K_i \ne 0$, the setpoint $r$ is constant, and $y_m = y + b$ is the measurement of the controlled variable $y$ with a constant sensor error $b$. Then at every equilibrium of the closed loop $y_m = r$, that is, $y = r - b$ — whatever the plant, the other terms of the controller and the constant disturbances.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At an equilibrium every derivative vanishes, in particular $\dot I = K_i(r - y_m) = 0$.
\end{proof}

The theorem says nothing about whether an equilibrium exists or is ever reached; \lessonref{les:droop} adds those hypotheses. It is the exact form of the warning above: the sensor error $b$ passes to the output undiminished.

\begin{theorem}[Accuracy of the discrete integral and derivative]
\label{thm:meet-discrete}
Let $e$ be twice continuously differentiable on $[0, t_N]$ and $t_k = k\,\Delta t$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \Big|\sum_{k=1}^{N} e(t_k)\,\Delta t - \int_0^{t_N} e(\tau)\,\dd\tau\Big| &\le \tfrac12\,\Delta t\;t_N\,\max|\dot e| , \\
  \Big|\frac{e(t_k) - e(t_{k-1})}{\Delta t} - \dot e(t_k)\Big| &\le \tfrac12\,\Delta t\,\max|\ddot e| .
\end{align*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On the interval $[t_{k-1}, t_k]$, by the mean value theorem, $|e(t_k) - e(\tau)| \le \max|\dot e|\,(t_k - \tau)$. Integrating this over the interval bounds its share of the error by $\tfrac12\max|\dot e|\,\Delta t^2$, and there are $N = t_N/\Delta t$ intervals. For the derivative, Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder gives, for some $\xi$ in the interval,
\begin{equation*}
  e(t_{k-1}) = e(t_k) - \dot e(t_k)\,\Delta t + \tfrac12\,\ddot e(\xi)\,\Delta t^2 .
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Both errors shrink in proportion to $\Delta t$: the two rules are \emph{first-order accurate}.\tip{Halve the step and the error halves: that is what \enquote{first-order} means. For a second-order rule, such as the trapezoid, it would fall to a quarter.} With $\Delta t = \SI{4}{ms}$ and an error whose second derivative stays below the drone's largest acceleration, about \SI{15}{m/s^2}, the derivative is wrong by at most \SI{0.03}{m/s}, which $K_d = 7$ turns into \SI{0.2}{N}.

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The L1 drone obeys $m\ddot y = T - mg + d$: a double integrator. A constant force error grows quadratically into a position error.
  \item Open-loop control computes a command from a model; every model error ends in a drift.
  \item Feedback measures the result, compares it with the setpoint and acts on the error. Even a pure proportional correction turns an unbounded drift into a bounded error $(m - \hat m)g/K_p$.
  \item The PID controller combines three views of the error: present (P), past (I), future (D) — plus a feedforward guess.
  \item A real controller runs in discrete steps: a running sum for the integral, a difference quotient for the derivative.
  \item The state of the drone is $(y, v)$: two numbers that, with the forces, fix its future.
  \item Feedback corrects what enters after the comparison. It cannot correct its own sensor, and it acts only once an error exists.
  \item A prediction is tested by flying it. Where model and flight disagree, the shape of the error says what the model left out.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{The governor that started it all}
\sidephoto{governor}{52mm}{A centrifugal governor by Boulton \& Watt. As the shaft spins faster, the balls fly outward and close the steam valve.}
In 1788 James Watt\index{centrifugal governor}\index{Watt, James} fitted his steam engines with a device borrowed from windmills: two heavy balls on hinged arms, spun by the engine. When the engine ran too fast, the balls swung outward and, through a linkage, closed the steam valve; when it slowed, they fell and opened it. It is a proportional controller made of iron — measure (the ball height), compare (with the linkage geometry), act (on the valve) — and it made the steam engine usable in factories.

Governors had a curious habit, though: some of them \emph{hunted}, oscillating around the desired speed instead of settling. In 1868 James Clerk Maxwell\index{Maxwell, James Clerk} — the same Maxwell of electromagnetism — published \emph{On Governors}, the first mathematical analysis of a feedback loop. He linearised the equations of motion and showed that stability depends on the roots of a characteristic polynomial: if any root has a positive real part, the governor hunts. That is exactly the tool we will use in \lessonref{les:spring} and \lessonref{les:damper}.

Eighty years later Norbert Wiener\index{Wiener, Norbert} named his new science \emph{cybernetics} partly in homage to Maxwell's paper. Today the same loop you see in \cref{fig:meet-loop} runs in your car's engine, your phone's camera stabiliser, the thermostat on your wall, and in every one of the hundreds of millions of drones sold so far.
\end{curiosity}
