\section{The closed loop is a differential equation}

In this lesson the altitude controller is reduced to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-law}
  T = \hat m g + K_p\,(r - y),
\end{equation}
the law we met in \cref{eq:meet-p}, with a feedforward that is exactly right, $\hat m = m$. What does the drone do under this law? To find out, we substitute the law into the plant \eqref{eq:meet-plant}. With no wind ($d = 0$):
\begin{equation}
  m\ddot y = \underbrace{\hat m g + K_p(r-y)}_{T} - mg = K_p\,(r - y).
\end{equation}
The two weights cancel. The controller and the drone have merged into a single equation. It is worth pausing on this, because it is the method of the whole book:

\begin{keyidea}[The closed loop is a new system]
A controller and a plant, connected, form a new dynamical system with its own equation. To understand a feedback loop is to understand that equation. Most of this book consists of writing it down and reading it.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Measuring from the setpoint}
The equation becomes cleaner if we measure the altitude from the setpoint. Define the deviation
\begin{equation}
  x(t) = y(t) - r .
\end{equation}
For a constant setpoint, $\dot x = \dot y$ and $\ddot x = \ddot y$, and $r - y = -x$. Substituting:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-ode}
  m\,\ddot x + K_p\,x = 0 .
\end{equation}
This is the equation of a mass on a spring of stiffness $K_p$. The proportional term is literally a spring stretched between the drone and the setpoint: its force $-K_p x$ is proportional to the stretch and points back towards the setpoint.

\section{Solving the equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:spring-ode} is a \emph{linear ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients}. Such equations have a standard method of solution, and because we will use it in almost every lesson, we go through it carefully once.

\subsection{Step 1: guess an exponential}
We look for solutions of the form $x(t) = e^{st}$, with $s$ a number to be found. The reason for this guess: differentiating an exponential gives back the same exponential, multiplied by $s$. So $\dot x = s\,e^{st}$ and $\ddot x = s^2 e^{st}$, and every term of the equation will contain the same factor $e^{st}$.

\subsection{Step 2: the characteristic equation}
Substitute the guess into \cref{eq:spring-ode}:
\begin{equation}
  m s^2 e^{st} + K_p\,e^{st} = (m s^2 + K_p)\,e^{st} = 0 .
\end{equation}
An exponential is never zero, so the bracket must vanish:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-char}
  m s^2 + K_p = 0 .
\end{equation}
This is the \term{characteristic equation} of the system. Its left-hand side, the \term{characteristic polynomial}, is obtained from the differential equation by replacing each derivative $\frac{\dd^n}{\dd t^n}$ with $s^n$.

\subsection{Step 3: the roots}
Solve for $s$: $s^2 = -K_p/m$, so
\begin{equation}
  s_{1,2} = \pm\, j\,\omega_n,\qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{K_p/m},
\end{equation}
where $j = \sqrt{-1}$ is the imaginary unit (engineers write $j$, because $i$ is taken by electric current). The roots are purely imaginary. For the default $K_p = 10$ and $m = 1$: $\omega_n = \sqrt{10} = \SI{3.16}{rad/s}$.

\subsection{Step 4: from complex exponentials to sines and cosines}
Both $e^{j\omega_n t}$ and $e^{-j\omega_n t}$ solve the equation, and because it is linear, so does any combination of them. They are complex, but Euler's formula,
\begin{equation}
  e^{j\omega t} = \cos\omega t + j\sin\omega t ,
\end{equation}
shows that their sum and difference give real functions: $\cos\omega_n t = \tfrac12(e^{j\omega_n t} + e^{-j\omega_n t})$ and $\sin\omega_n t = \tfrac1{2j}(e^{j\omega_n t} - e^{-j\omega_n t})$. So the general real solution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-general}
  x(t) = A\cos\omega_n t + B\sin\omega_n t ,
\end{equation}
with two constants $A$ and $B$ — two, because the equation is of second order and needs two initial conditions.

\subsection{Step 5: fit the initial conditions}
At $t = 0$: $x(0) = A$ and $\dot x(0) = B\,\omega_n$. So if the drone starts at deviation $x_0$ with velocity $v_0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-sol}
  x(t) = x_0 \cos\omega_n t + \frac{v_0}{\omega_n}\sin\omega_n t .
\end{equation}
It is an oscillation with the \term{natural frequency} $\omega_n$ and the period
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-period}
  T_{osc} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_n} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{K_p}}.
\end{equation}
Nothing in this solution decays.

\begin{example}[The setpoint step of the lesson]
\label{ex:spring-step}
The drone hovers at \SI{2}{m} when, at $t = \SI{6}{s}$, the setpoint jumps to \SI{3}{m}. With $K_p = 10$, $m = \SI{1}{kg}$, describe the motion completely.

\textbf{Step 1 — initial conditions.} Measure time from the jump. The new setpoint is $r = 3$, the drone is at $y = 2$ and at rest, so $x_0 = y - r = -1$\,m and $v_0 = 0$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the solution.} From \cref{eq:spring-sol}: $x(t) = -\cos(3.16\,t)$, i.e.\ $y(t) = 3 - \cos(3.16\,t)$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the range.} The cosine runs between $-1$ and $1$, so $y$ swings between \SI{2}{m} and \SI{4}{m}: the drone overshoots the new setpoint by as much as it was below it.

\textbf{Step 4 — the period.} $T_{osc} = 2\pi/3.16 = \SI{1.99}{s}$.

\textbf{Step 5 — the speed.} $\dot y(t) = 3.16\,\sin(3.16\,t)$, largest when the drone passes the setpoint: \SI{3.16}{m/s}.

\textbf{Step 6 — the thrust.} $T = mg - K_p x = 9.81 + 10\cos(3.16\,t)$: between $-0.19$ and \SI{19.81}{N}. The lower end is slightly negative — impossible for propellers, so the motors clip briefly at zero at the top of each swing. Everything else is within the \SI{24.4}{N} limit.
\end{example}

\subsection{Poles and stability}
The roots of the characteristic equation are so important that they have their own name.

\begin{definition}{Poles and stability}
For a linear system the roots $s$ of the characteristic equation are called its \term{poles}. Every solution is a combination of $e^{st}$ over the poles. Writing a pole as $s = \sigma + j\omega$, the corresponding motion is $e^{\sigma t}(\cos\omega t + j\sin\omega t)$: the imaginary part $\omega$ is a frequency of oscillation, the real part $\sigma$ a rate of growth ($\sigma > 0$) or decay ($\sigma < 0$). Hence:
\begin{itemize}
  \item if every pole has $\operatorname{Re}s < 0$, every solution decays: the system is \term{asymptotically stable};
  \item if some pole has $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$, some solution grows without bound: \term{unstable};
  \item poles on the imaginary axis (and none to the right) give oscillations that neither grow nor decay: \term{marginally stable}.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Three poles, three behaviours]
What does a pole at $s = -1$, at $s = +0.15$ and at $s = \pm 3.16j$ mean for the motion, starting from $x = 1$?

\textbf{$s = -1$.} $x(t) = e^{-t}$: after \SI{1}{s} it is $0.37$, after \SI{3}{s} $0.05$. Decay with time constant \SI{1}{s}.

\textbf{$s = +0.15$.} $x(t) = e^{0.15t}$: after \SI{5}{s} it is $2.1$, after \SI{20}{s} $20$. Slow, relentless growth — unstable.

\textbf{$s = \pm3.16j$.} $x(t) = \cos 3.16t$: it oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ forever. Marginally stable: our drone.
\end{example}

A P-controlled hover is marginally stable, the most fragile case of all: the smallest push of the poles to the right — by a delay, a lag — turns it into an unstable one. We will see the motors do exactly that in a moment.

\section{Why a spring never stops}

There is a second way to see the endless oscillation, and it explains much more than the formula. Multiply \cref{eq:spring-ode} by the velocity $\dot x$:
\begin{equation}
  m\dot x\ddot x + K_p x\dot x = 0 .
\end{equation}
By the chain rule, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(\tfrac12\dot x^2\big) = \dot x\ddot x$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(\tfrac12 x^2\big) = x\dot x$, so the equation says
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big(\underbrace{\tfrac12 m\dot x^2}_{\text{kinetic}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 K_p x^2}_{\text{spring}}\Big) = 0 .
\end{equation}
The energy $E = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2$ is conserved. The setpoint jump stretches the \enquote{spring} and stores $\tfrac12 K_p x_0^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 1^2 = \SI{5}{J}$ in it; the loop then only moves that energy back and forth between the spring and the motion (\cref{fig:spring-energy}). As a check: when all of it is kinetic, $\tfrac12 m v^2 = 5$ gives $v = \SI{3.16}{m/s}$ — the peak speed of Example~\ref{ex:spring-step}.\marginfig{spring_energy}{In an undamped oscillator the energy sloshes between the spring and the motion; the total never changes.}{fig:spring-energy}

\begin{keyidea}[Stiffness is not damping]
A proportional force depends only on \emph{position}. Such a force can store energy but never remove it. To bring the drone to rest, something must act against the \emph{velocity} — a damper. Raising $K_p$ makes the spring stiffer and the oscillation faster, but not smaller.
\end{keyidea}

An intuition for the same fact: when the drone passes the setpoint on its way up, the error is zero, so the P term is zero — it does not \enquote{know} that the drone is moving at full speed. Only after the drone has overshot does P start to pull it back, and by then it is too late to stop it gently. A pilot would throttle back \emph{before} reaching the target; P cannot, because it cannot see velocity.

\section{What the simulator says}

\simfig{spring_run}{The spring lesson flown in the simulator. With $K_p=10$ (blue) the drone oscillates with the predicted period of \SI{2.0}{s}; the peaks shrink very slowly. With $K_p = 40$ (orange) the thrust (bottom) hits both motor limits on every swing — the loop is no longer linear, and the linear prediction of a \SI{1.0}{s} period fails.}{fig:spring-run}

\Cref{fig:spring-run} shows the lesson's run. For $K_p = 10$ the prediction is excellent: the drone swings from \SI{2}{m} to \SI{3.8}{m} and the period is \SI{2.0}{s}, as in Example~\ref{ex:spring-step}. The oscillation is not \emph{perfectly} undamped, though: the peaks sink from \SI{3.80}{m} to \SI{3.49}{m} over six periods, and more and more slowly. Two small effects that \cref{eq:spring-ode} ignores are fighting each other here.\aside{The info card in the app still says \enquote{undamped}: it analyses the controller, and the controller alone adds no damping.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Air drag removes energy.} The simulator's drag $F = -c_v|v|v$ always opposes the motion. It is quadratic, so it matters a lot for fast swings and hardly at all for slow ones.
  \item \textbf{Motor lag adds energy.} The motors follow the command with a time constant $\tau_m = \SI{30}{ms}$, so the spring force arrives slightly late: $F(t) \approx -K_p\,x(t - \tau_m)$. A first-order Taylor expansion, $x(t - \tau_m) \approx x(t) - \tau_m\dot x(t)$, gives
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-lag}
    F \approx -K_p\,x + K_p\tau_m\,\dot x ,
  \end{equation}
  a spring plus a damper with a \emph{negative} coefficient $-K_p\tau_m$. A late spring pushes slightly in the direction of motion and pumps energy in.
\end{itemize}
For large swings drag wins and the oscillation shrinks; as it shrinks, drag weakens, until the two balance and the drone settles into a steady oscillation, a \term{limit cycle}. No linear model can produce one. The balance can even be predicted.

\begin{example}[Predicting the size of the limit cycle]
\label{ex:spring-limit}
Estimate the amplitude at which drag and motor lag balance, for $K_p = 10$.

\textbf{Step 1 — assume the motion.} Near the balance the oscillation is almost sinusoidal: $x = A\cos\omega t$, $\dot x = -A\omega\sin\omega t$, with $\omega = \omega_n = \SI{3.16}{rad/s}$.

\textbf{Step 2 — energy pumped in per period by the lag.} The force $+K_p\tau_m\dot x$ does work $\int F\,\dot x\,\dd t = K_p\tau_m\int\dot x^2\,\dd t$ over one period $2\pi/\omega$. Since the average of $\sin^2$ is $\tfrac12$: $W_{in} = K_p\tau_m\,A^2\omega^2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{2\pi}{\omega} = \pi K_p\tau_m A^2\omega$.

\textbf{Step 3 — energy removed per period by drag.} $W_{out} = \int c_v|\dot x|\dot x^2\,\dd t = c_v A^3\omega^3\int|\sin|^3\,\dd t$. Over one period the average of $|\sin|^3$ is $\tfrac{4}{3\pi}$, so $W_{out} = c_v A^3\omega^3\cdot\tfrac{4}{3\pi}\cdot\tfrac{2\pi}{\omega} = \tfrac83\,c_v A^3\omega^2$.

\textbf{Step 4 — balance.} $\pi K_p\tau_m A^2\omega = \tfrac83\,c_v A^3\omega^2$ gives
\begin{equation*}
  A = \frac{3\pi\,K_p\,\tau_m}{8\,c_v\,\omega}.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 5 — numbers.} With $K_p = 10$, $\tau_m = \SI{0.03}{s}$ plus about \SI{2}{ms} of controller sampling (Lesson 9) — $\tau \approx \SI{0.032}{s}$ — $c_v = 0.25$ and $\omega = 3.16$: $A = 3\pi\cdot0.32/(8\cdot0.25\cdot3.16) = 3.02/6.32 = \SI{0.48}{m}$.

\textbf{Step 6 — check.} In \cref{fig:spring-run} the peaks at the end of the twenty-second run are at \SI{3.49}{m}, i.e.\ an amplitude of \SI{0.49}{m}, and still sinking. Flown for a full minute (\cref{fig:spring-predict}), the oscillation settles at \SI{0.46}{m}. A back-of-the-envelope energy balance predicts a nonlinear effect of the simulator to within 4\,\%.
\end{example}

\subsection{When the linear model stops being true}
Now the orange curve. \Cref{eq:spring-period} promises that quadrupling $K_p$ halves the period, to \SI{1.0}{s}. The simulator shows \SI{1.4}{s} — and an oscillation that does not decay at all.

Look at the thrust. After a \SI{1}{m} step the P term asks for $K_p\,e = \SI{40}{N}$ on top of the weight, but four motors can deliver at most $4 \times \SI{6.1}{N} = \SI{24.4}{N}$, and at least zero. On every swing the command is \emph{clipped} at one limit or the other. While it is clipped, the drone does not feel a spring of stiffness 40 at all; it feels a constant force. The effective spring is softer, so the period is longer. And the negative damping of \cref{eq:spring-lag} is four times stronger than at $K_p=10$, so the oscillation is held at the size where the motor limits and the drag stop it from growing.

\begin{watchout}[Linear results need linear conditions]
Every formula in this lesson — $\omega_n$, the period, the pole positions — assumes that the actuators do what they are told. Large steps and large gains break that assumption. The motors of Anemostatos, like all real actuators, have limits; \lessonref{les:windup} is devoted to what that does to the integral term.
\end{watchout}

To see the linear behaviour at $K_p = 40$, keep the step small: press \key{↑} once (\SI{0.1}{m}) instead of jumping a whole metre. The thrust then stays within its limits and the period comes out close to \SI{1}{s}.

\section{The engineer's view: state, true poles, limit cycle}

\subsection{The loop in state form}
In \lessonref{les:meet} we wrote the plant with its state, the position and the velocity. The closed loop \eqref{eq:spring-ode} has the same state, $(x, \dot x)$, and in that form it reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-state}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \dot x \end{pmatrix}
  = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -K_p/m & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{A}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \dot x \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad\text{in short}\quad \dot{\vx} = A\,\vx \quad\text{with}\quad \vx = (x, \dot x)^\T .
\end{equation}
Try a solution $e^{st}\symbf v$ with a constant vector $\symbf v$. Differentiating multiplies it by $s$, so $s\,\symbf v = A\symbf v$: the number $s$ must be an eigenvalue of $A$. The eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(s\Id - A) = s^2 + K_p/m$, which is the characteristic equation \eqref{eq:spring-char} once more.

\begin{result}[Poles are eigenvalues]
Write a linear system as $\dot{\vx} = A\vx$. Its poles are the eigenvalues of the state matrix $A$, and its characteristic polynomial is $\det(s\Id - A)$. For two states this is a second way to write what we already had. For twelve it is the only practical way, and it is how the simulator computes the poles it displays for the controllers of Part~II (\src{src/math/mat.ts}, function \texttt{eigenvalues}).
\end{result}

The plane with $x$ on one axis and $\dot x$ on the other is the \term{phase plane}. A solution is a curve in it, traced as time passes. Energy conservation, $\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2 = E$, says that the curves of the ideal spring are ellipses: the drone circles the setpoint for ever, neither closer nor further. In the app, the bottom-right chart can be switched to \ui{phase portrait} to watch the curve being drawn.

\subsection{The true poles}
The ideal loop sits exactly on the boundary of stability. \Cref{eq:spring-lag} suggested that the motor lag pushes it over. With the state form we can say so exactly.

\begin{example}[The proportional loop with its motors, exactly]
\label{ex:spring-cubic}
Model the motors as a first-order lag with $\tau = \SI{30}{ms}$ and find the poles of the P loop for $K_p = 10$, $m = \SI{1}{kg}$.

\textbf{Step 1 — the motor.} Let $F$ be the force the motors actually deliver beyond the weight. It follows the command $-K_p x$ with a time constant $\tau$: $\tau\,\dot F = -F - K_p x$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the drone.} $m\ddot x = F$. The system now has three state variables, $x$, $\dot x$ and $F$.

\textbf{Step 3 — one equation.} Differentiate Newton's law and insert the motor: $m\dddot x = \dot F = -(F + K_p x)/\tau = -(m\ddot x + K_p x)/\tau$. Multiply by $\tau$:
\begin{equation*}
  \tau m\,\dddot x + m\,\ddot x + K_p\,x = 0,
  \qquad\text{with the characteristic equation}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
  \tau m\,s^3 + m\,s^2 + K_p = 0 .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 4 — a test without solving.} If all three roots had negative real parts, the polynomial would be a product of factors $(s + a)$ and $(s^2 + bs + c)$ with positive $a$, $b$, $c$, and multiplying such factors out gives only positive coefficients. Ours has no $s$ term at all: its coefficient is zero. So at least one root is not in the left half-plane — for \emph{every} positive $K_p$ and $\tau$. No choice of gain makes this loop stable. (\Cref{thm:spring-routh} below states the test in full.)

\textbf{Step 5 — the roots.} Numerically, for $0.03\,s^3 + s^2 + 10 = 0$:
\begin{equation*}
  s_1 = -33.63, \qquad s_{2,3} = +0.147 \pm 3.145\,j .
\end{equation*}
(Check: the roots of a cubic sum to minus the ratio of its first two coefficients, $-1/\tau = -33.33$, and $-33.63 + 2\cdot0.147 = -33.33$.)

\textbf{Step 6 — read them.} The real pole is the motor itself, a little faster than $-1/\tau$. The complex pair is the spring, at almost the old frequency (\SI{3.145}{rad/s} instead of 3.162), but with a \emph{positive} real part: the oscillation grows like $e^{0.147t}$ and doubles every $\ln 2/0.147 = \SI{4.7}{s}$. The first-order estimate of \cref{eq:spring-lag} gives $K_p\tau/(2m) = 0.15$, which is the same number.
\end{example}

So the honest statement is stronger than \enquote{marginally stable}: a proportional loop around real motors is \emph{unstable}. Then why does the drone of \cref{fig:spring-run} not fly away?

\subsection{The amplitude equation}
Because the growth is slow and the air resists more the faster the drone moves. Example~\ref{ex:spring-limit} balanced the two over one period. The same two energies give more than the balance point: they give the whole history of the amplitude. With $m\omega^2 = K_p$, an oscillation of amplitude $A$ carries the energy $E = \tfrac12 K_p A^2$. Per period it gains $W_{in} - W_{out}$; divided by the period $2\pi/\omega$, that is its average rate of change:
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\dd E}{\dd t} = K_p A\,\dot A = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}\Big(\pi K_p\tau A^2\omega - \tfrac83\,c_v A^3\omega^2\Big).
\end{equation*}
Dividing by $K_p A$ and using $\omega^2 = K_p/m$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spring-amp}
  \dot A = \sigma A\Big(1 - \frac{A}{A^*}\Big), \qquad
  \sigma = \frac{K_p\tau}{2m}, \qquad
  A^* = \frac{3\pi K_p\tau}{8\,c_v\,\omega}.
\end{equation}
This is the logistic equation, and it can be read without solving it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A small oscillation, $A \ll A^*$, grows exponentially at the rate $\sigma$ — the real part of the unstable poles of Example~\ref{ex:spring-cubic}.
  \item A large one, $A > A^*$, shrinks: the bracket is negative.
  \item At $A = A^*$ it stays. Every oscillation, from either side, ends there.
\end{itemize}
Its solution from an amplitude $A_0$ is $A(t) = A^*\big/\big(1 + (A^*/A_0 - 1)\,e^{-\sigma t}\big)$. With $\tau = \SI{32}{ms}$ (motor lag plus half a sample): $\sigma = \SI{0.16}{s^{-1}}$ and $A^* = \SI{0.48}{m}$.

\simfig{spring_predict}{Two flights of a minute each, one after a \SI{1}{m} step and one after a \SI{0.1}{m} step. Left: the height of every peak above the setpoint (dots) and \cref{eq:spring-amp} started from the second peak (dashed). Right: both flights in the phase plane, with the predicted limit cycle (dashed ellipse). The large oscillation winds inwards and the small one outwards, onto the same curve.}{fig:spring-predict}

\Cref{fig:spring-predict} is the test. After a large step the oscillation shrinks; after a small one it \emph{grows}, by itself, out of a drone that was almost at rest. Both end at \SI{0.46}{m}. Fitting \cref{eq:spring-amp} to the measured peaks gives $\sigma = \SI{0.152}{s^{-1}}$ and $A^* = \SI{0.46}{m}$, against the predicted 0.16 and 0.48.

\begin{keyidea}[Linear analysis says whether, nonlinear analysis says how far]
The poles of the linear model decide whether a small motion grows or dies. They cannot say where a growing motion ends, because in a linear model it never ends. The end is set by whatever stops being linear first — here the air drag, at $K_p = 40$ the motor limits. A steady oscillation that attracts its neighbours from both sides is called a \term{stable limit cycle}, and it is one of the signatures of a nonlinear loop.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{example}[The same equation in orbit]
\label{ex:spring-sat}
A small satellite with a moment of inertia $J = \SI{12}{kg.m^2}$ about one axis is turned by a reaction wheel. Its pointing error $\theta$ is fed back proportionally, torque $= -K_p\theta$ with $K_p = \SI{0.3}{N.m/rad}$. What does it do?

\textbf{Step 1 — the equation.} Newton's law for rotation is $J\ddot\theta = $ torque, so $J\ddot\theta + K_p\theta = 0$: \cref{eq:spring-ode} with $J$ in place of $m$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the motion.} $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_p/J} = \sqrt{0.3/12} = \SI{0.158}{rad/s}$, a period of \SI{39.7}{s}. A pointing error of \SI{1}{\degree} becomes an oscillation of $\pm\SI{1}{\degree}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the actuator.} A wheel that follows its command with $\tau = \SI{0.2}{s}$ adds the growth rate $\sigma = K_p\tau/(2J) = 0.3\cdot0.2/24 = \SI{0.0025}{s^{-1}}$: the error doubles every $\ln2/0.0025 = \SI{280}{s}$.

\textbf{Step 4 — and nothing stops it.} In orbit there is no air, so there is no drag term and no limit cycle: the oscillation grows until the wheel saturates. This is why no spacecraft is pointed by a proportional law alone. Every attitude controller also feeds back the turn rate measured by gyroscopes, which is the damper of \lessonref{les:damper}.
\end{example}

\subsection{In formal terms}
This lesson used the words stable and unstable for where the poles lie. The literature defines them for any system, linear or not, and the pole condition becomes a theorem.

\begin{definition}{Stability of an equilibrium, after Lyapunov}
Let $\vx^*$ be an equilibrium of $\dot{\vx} = f(\vx)$, that is, $f(\vx^*) = 0$. It is
\begin{itemize}
  \item \term{stable} if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $\|\vx(0) - \vx^*\| < \delta$ implies $\|\vx(t) - \vx^*\| < \varepsilon$ for all $t \ge 0$;
  \item \term{asymptotically stable} if it is stable and there is a $\delta_0 > 0$ such that $\|\vx(0) - \vx^*\| < \delta_0$ implies $\vx(t) \to \vx^*$ as $t \to \infty$;
  \item \term{globally asymptotically stable} if it is stable and $\vx(t) \to \vx^*$ from every initial state;
  \item \term{unstable} if it is not stable.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

In words: start close enough and you stay as close as you like (stable), and moreover you return (asymptotically stable). A system that oscillates for ever with a constant amplitude is stable in this sense. \enquote{Marginally stable} means stable but not asymptotically stable.

\begin{theorem}[Stability of a linear system]
\label{thm:spring-linear}
Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ be the eigenvalues of $A$. The equilibrium $\vx = 0$ of $\dot{\vx} = A\vx$ is
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item asymptotically stable if and only if $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i < 0$ for every $i$; it is then globally so, and there are constants $c, \alpha > 0$ with $\|\vx(t)\| \le c\,e^{-\alpha t}\|\vx(0)\|$;
  \item stable if and only if $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i \le 0$ for every $i$ and every eigenvalue with zero real part has as many independent eigenvectors as its multiplicity;
  \item unstable in every other case.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors $\symbf v_i$, every solution is $\sum_i c_i\,e^{\lambda_i t}\symbf v_i$ by \cref{thm:meet-solution}, and $|e^{\lambda_i t}| = e^{\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i\,t}$, from which the three cases follow. A matrix without a full set of eigenvectors adds terms $t^k e^{\lambda_i t}$; they still decay when $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i < 0$ and grow when $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_i = 0$. The general proof uses the Jordan form of $A$ (Khalil, 2002, ch.~4).
\end{proof}

The theorem sorts the three systems we have met. The drone alone, \cref{eq:meet-state}, has the eigenvalue $0$ twice and only one eigenvector, $(1, 0)^\T$: case (iii), \emph{unstable} — a drone given the slightest velocity drifts away as $v_0 t$. The ideal P loop has $\pm j\omega_n$, each once: case (ii), stable but not asymptotically. The P loop with its motors has a pair with real part $+0.147$: unstable.

\begin{theorem}[Routh–Hurwitz, degrees two and three]
\label{thm:spring-routh}
Let the coefficients be real and the leading one positive.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item All roots of $a_2 s^2 + a_1 s + a_0$ have negative real parts if and only if $a_1 > 0$ and $a_0 > 0$.
  \item All roots of $a_3 s^3 + a_2 s^2 + a_1 s + a_0$ have negative real parts if and only if $a_2 > 0$, $a_0 > 0$ and $a_2 a_1 > a_3 a_0$.
  \item For a polynomial of any degree, coefficients that are all positive are necessary.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Divide by the leading coefficient. (i)~The roots have the sum $-a_1$ and the product $a_0$. Two real roots are both negative exactly when their sum is negative and their product positive; a complex pair has the real part $-a_1/2$ and a positive product.
(ii)~A real cubic has a real root, $-a$, so it factors as $(s + a)(s^2 + bs + c)$ with $a_2 = a + b$, $a_1 = ab + c$, $a_0 = ac$, and by (i) all roots lie in the left half-plane exactly when $a, b, c > 0$. A short calculation gives $a_2 a_1 - a_0 = b\,(a\,a_2 + c)$. If $a, b, c > 0$, the three conditions hold. Conversely, $a_0 = ac > 0$ makes $a$ and $c$ of one sign. Were both negative, then $a\,a_2 + c < 0$ and $b = a_2 - a > 0$, so $a_2 a_1 - a_0 < 0$, a contradiction. Hence $a, c > 0$, so $a\,a_2 + c > 0$ and therefore $b > 0$.
(iii)~A polynomial with all roots in the left half-plane is a product of factors $(s + a)$ and $(s^2 + bs + c)$ with positive $a, b, c$, and such a product has positive coefficients.
\end{proof}

Step~4 of Example~\ref{ex:spring-cubic} is part (ii) with $a_1 = 0$: then $a_2 a_1 = 0 < a_3 a_0$ for every positive gain and lag. \Lessonref{les:integral} applies the same part to $m s^3 + K_d s^2 + K_p s + K_i$ and finds $K_i < K_p K_d/m$.

\begin{theorem}[Stability from the linearisation]
\label{thm:spring-indirect}
Let $f$ be continuously differentiable, $f(\vx^*) = 0$, and let $A$ be the matrix of partial derivatives of $f$ at $\vx^*$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item If every eigenvalue of $A$ has a negative real part, $\vx^*$ is an asymptotically stable equilibrium of the nonlinear system $\dot{\vx} = f(\vx)$.
  \item If at least one eigenvalue has a positive real part, $\vx^*$ is unstable.
  \item If no eigenvalue has a positive real part but some lie on the imaginary axis, $A$ decides nothing: the nonlinear terms do.
\end{enumerate}
This is Lyapunov's indirect method; for a proof see Khalil (2002, ch.~4).
\end{theorem}

This theorem is the licence for analysing a nonlinear drone with linear tools, and it marks where the licence ends. The real P loop is case (ii): the hover is unstable however small the disturbance, and the air drag cannot change that, because $c_v|v|v$ has no linear part — its derivative at $v = 0$ is zero. The ideal P loop is case (iii), and there the drag does decide: with drag and no lag the energy obeys $\dot E = -c_v|\dot x|^3 \le 0$ and the drone comes to rest after all, which the linear model could not know.

One more hypothesis deserves its name. \Cref{eq:spring-amp} was obtained by averaging over one period, which is legitimate when the amplitude changes little within a period, $\sigma \ll \omega$. Here $\sigma/\omega = 0.16/3.16 = 0.05$, and the method of averaging bounds the error by a quantity of that order (Khalil, 2002, ch.~10). The measured $\sigma$ and $A^*$ differ from the predicted ones by 4 to 5\,\%.

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item With P alone (and exact feedforward) the closed loop is $m\ddot x + K_p x = 0$: a mass on a spring of stiffness $K_p$.
  \item Linear ODEs with constant coefficients are solved with $e^{st}$: the characteristic equation gives the poles, the initial conditions fix the constants.
  \item The P loop's poles are $\pm j\omega_n$, $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_p/m}$: marginally stable, oscillating forever with period $2\pi/\omega_n$.
  \item A force that depends on position alone conserves energy. Only a force that depends on velocity can remove it, and a lagging force adds it.
  \item Poles are the eigenvalues of the state matrix. With the motor lag included, the P loop has a pair in the right half-plane: it is unstable for every gain.
  \item Drag limits the growth. The amplitude obeys $\dot A = \sigma A(1 - A/A^*)$ and ends on a limit cycle from either side.
  \item Linear predictions hold only while the actuators stay within their limits.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{Galloping Gertie}
\photo{tacoma}{The Tacoma Narrows Bridge twisting on 7 November 1940, an hour before it fell into Puget Sound.}
The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State opened on 1 July 1940. It was elegant and very slender, and from the first day it moved: in moderate wind the deck rose and fell by a metre or more, and drivers came from far away to ride \enquote{Galloping Gertie}. On the morning of 7 November, in a steady wind of about \SI{65}{km/h}, the motion changed into a violent twisting mode. It grew for an hour, and then the main span tore apart.

It is often told as a story of resonance — the wind \enquote{hitting the natural frequency}. The true story is more interesting for us. A steady wind has no frequency to resonate with. What happened is that the air flowing around the twisting deck pushed it in phase with its own velocity: the aerodynamic force acted like a damper with a \emph{negative} coefficient — exactly like the late spring of \cref{eq:spring-lag}. This is called aeroelastic flutter. Instead of removing energy, the air pumped energy in on every cycle.

In the language of this lesson, the poles of the bridge moved from just left of the imaginary axis to just right of it. That is why engineers since then have cared so much about damping, and why wind-tunnel tests of bridge models became standard practice.
\end{curiosity}
