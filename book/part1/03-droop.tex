\section{The loop without feedforward}

In this lesson the thrust is made of the proportional and derivative terms only:
\begin{equation}
  T = K_p\,e - K_d\,\dot y ,\qquad e = r - y .
\end{equation}
(The D term here acts on the measured velocity rather than on $\dot e$; for a constant setpoint the two are the same, $\dot e = -\dot y$, and we will see in \lessonref{les:kick} why this form is preferred.) Substituting into the plant \eqref{eq:meet-plant} with no wind:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:droop-loop}
  m\ddot y = K_p\,(r - y) - K_d\,\dot y - m g .
\end{equation}
The weight no longer cancels. Something must carry it.

\section{Where can the drone rest?}

We are not asking yet how the drone moves, only where it can stop. Such a point has its own name.

\begin{definition}{Equilibrium and steady state}
An \term{equilibrium} of a dynamical system is a state in which it can remain forever: all time derivatives are zero. If the system is stable, it approaches an equilibrium after a disturbance or a setpoint change; the values it approaches are its \term{steady state}, written with the subscript \emph{ss}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[The droop, step by step]
\label{ex:droop-ess}
Find the equilibrium of \cref{eq:droop-loop} for $m = \SI{1}{kg}$, $K_p = 10$, $r = \SI{2}{m}$.

\textbf{Step 1 — set the derivatives to zero.} At rest $\dot y = 0$ and $\ddot y = 0$. The left-hand side vanishes, and so does the D term:\tip{Neither the damping nor the mass of the motion survives. A steady-state error depends only on what is left when every derivative is zero.}
$0 = K_p(r - y_{ss}) - mg$.

\textbf{Step 2 — solve for the error.} $K_p\,e_{ss} = mg$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:droop-ess}
  e_{ss} = \frac{mg}{K_p} = \frac{1 \cdot 9.81}{10} = \SI{0.981}{m}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 3 — the altitude.} $y_{ss} = r - e_{ss} = 2 - 0.981 = \SI{1.019}{m}$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the forces at rest.} The P term is $K_p e_{ss} = 10 \cdot 0.981 = \SI{9.81}{N}$ — exactly the weight. The D term is zero. The P term carries the whole drone.

\textbf{Step 5 — a stiffer spring.} With $K_p = 40$: $e_{ss} = 9.81/40 = \SI{0.245}{m}$, $y_{ss} = \SI{1.755}{m}$. Four times the gain, a quarter of the droop, but still a droop.
\end{example}

\Cref{fig:droop-run} confirms the prediction to the centimetre: the simulator's drone settles at \SI{1.019}{m} with $K_p = 10$ and at \SI{1.755}{m} with $K_p = 40$. On the right, the P term ends at \SI{9.81}{N} in both runs: it is doing the feedforward's job.

Right: the P term (dashed: $K_p=10$, solid: $K_p=40$) ends at \SI{9.81}{N} in both cases — the whole weight hangs on the spring.}{fig:droop-run}

\marginscreen{1905 675 0 112}{droop}{The lesson panel in the app, with the same prediction.}{fig:droop-panel}

There is a simple way to see why the droop is unavoidable. The drone is in a tug of war between gravity, which never tires, and the P term, which pulls only as hard as the error is large. The rope settles where the two pulls are equal — and since gravity pulls with \SI{9.81}{N}, the error must be large enough for P to pull with \SI{9.81}{N} too. Zero error would mean zero pull, and the drone would fall.

\begin{keyidea}[Steady-state error of a proportional loop]
Under a constant load $F$ that nothing else compensates, a proportional loop settles with the error
\begin{equation*}
  e_{ss} = F / K_p .
\end{equation*}
Stiffer springs sag less, but no finite stiffness removes the sag. The same holds for a constant wind (the load is the drag) or a wrong mass estimate (the load is the part of the weight that the feedforward misses).
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Why not just raise the gain?}
Equation~\eqref{eq:droop-ess} suggests an easy way out: make $K_p$ huge.\didyouknow{\emph{Droop} is a term of art. The generators of a power grid share the load through a deliberate proportional droop: each lets its speed sag by a few per cent between no load and full load.} To keep the droop below \SI{1}{cm} we would need $K_p = 9.81/0.01 = \SI{981}{N/m}$. In \lessonref{les:spring} we saw the first problem with large gains — the motors saturate and the loop oscillates harder. \Lessonref{les:edge} will show the fundamental one: the motors lag and the controller samples, and together they put an upper limit on any gain, around $K_p \approx 200$ for this drone. There are two better ways.

\section{Cure one: remember the error}

The integral term accumulates the error:
\begin{equation}
  I(t) = K_i\int_0^t e(\tau)\,\dd\tau ,\qquad \dot I = K_i\,e .
\end{equation}
As long as the drone hangs low, $e > 0$ and $I$ grows. It keeps growing until it can carry the weight by itself; only then can the error — and P — return to zero.\pitfall{The integral does not ask why the error is there. It winds up just as patiently against a wall, a dead motor or a hand holding the drone (\lessonref{les:windup}).}

\begin{result}[The integral can only rest at zero error]
At any equilibrium all derivatives vanish, in particular $\dot I = K_i\,e_{ss} = 0$. With $K_i \neq 0$ this forces $e_{ss} = 0$. The integral then holds the whole load: $I_{ss} = mg$ here.
\end{result}

\Lessonref{les:integral} is devoted to this idea; here we watch it work (\cref{fig:droop-ion}) and ask how long it takes.

\simfig{droop_ion}{At $t = \SI{10}{s}$ the integral is switched on. It slowly takes over the weight from the P term (bottom); the altitude creeps up to the setpoint (top). With the default $K_i = 0.8$ this takes a long time.}{fig:droop-ion}

\subsection{How fast does the integral pay back the load?}
The creep in \cref{fig:droop-ion} is slow: the drone moves a few centimetres per second at most. That suggests a simplification: treat it as \emph{quasi-static}, i.e.\ at every moment the drone is almost at rest, so its acceleration and velocity are negligible and the forces almost balance.

\begin{example}[The integral time constant]
\label{ex:droop-tau}
Derive how the error decays after the integral is switched on, and check the prediction against the simulator ten seconds later.

\textbf{Step 1 — the quasi-static balance.} With $\ddot y \approx 0$ and $\dot y \approx 0$, the forces balance: $K_p\,e + I = mg$.

\textbf{Step 2 — differentiate.} The weight is constant, so $K_p\,\dot e + \dot I = 0$.

\textbf{Step 3 — insert the integral's law.} $\dot I = K_i e$, hence $K_p\,\dot e = -K_i\,e$, or
\begin{equation*}
  \dot e = -\frac{K_i}{K_p}\,e .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 4 — solve.} This is the simplest differential equation there is: the rate of change is proportional to the value.\recall{$\dot e = -e/\tau$ has the solution $e_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$. Whenever a rate is proportional to the value, expect an exponential and look for its time constant.} Separating variables, $\dd e/e = -(K_i/K_p)\,\dd t$, and integrating from the moment $t_0$ when the integral is switched on,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:droop-tau}
  e(t) = e_{ss}\,e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_I},\qquad \tau_I = \frac{K_p}{K_i}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 5 — numbers.} $\tau_I = 10/0.8 = \SI{12.5}{s}$. Ten seconds after switching on: $e = 0.981\,e^{-10/12.5} = 0.981 \cdot 0.449 = \SI{0.44}{m}$. The simulator shows \SI{0.447}{m}.

\textbf{Step 6 — when is it done?} To shrink below \SI{1}{cm} the error must fall by a factor of 98: $e^{-t/12.5} = 1/98$, $t = 12.5\ln 98 = \SI{57}{s}$. With $K_i = 3$: $\tau_I = \SI{3.3}{s}$, done in \SI{15}{s}.
\end{example}

The time constant $K_p/K_i$ is exactly the \term{integral time} $T_i$ of the \enquote{standard form} of the PID that industrial controllers use,\aside{Standard form: $u = K_p\big(e + \tfrac{1}{T_i}\int e + T_d\,\dot e\big)$ with $T_i = K_p/K_i$ and $T_d = K_d/K_p$. It is the same controller with different knobs.} and it tells you how long the integral needs to pay back a load. It also explains why the quasi-static assumption worked: \SI{12.5}{s} is much slower than the spring–damper motion of the drone, which settles within a second or two.

A shortcut deserves one check against the full calculation. The tools are those of \lessonref{les:spring}.

\begin{example}[Checking the shortcut: the exact solution]
\label{ex:droop-exact}
Solve the creep of \cref{fig:droop-ion} without the quasi-static assumption ($m = 1$, $K_p = 10$, $K_d = 7$, $K_i = 0.8$) and compare with the simulator.

\textbf{Step 1 — the full equation.} After the switch, $m\ddot y = K_p e + I - K_d\dot y - mg$ with $\dot I = K_i e$. In terms of the error ($\dot y = -\dot e$): $m\ddot e + K_d\dot e + K_p e + I - mg = 0$. Differentiate once to remove the constant and insert $\dot I$:
\begin{equation*}
  m\,\dddot e + K_d\,\ddot e + K_p\,\dot e + K_i\,e = 0 .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 2 — the poles.} The characteristic equation $s^3 + 7s^2 + 10s + 0.8 = 0$ has the roots\tip{Check computed roots before using them: here their sum must be $-7$ and their product $-0.8$.}
\begin{equation*}
  s_1 = -5.052, \qquad s_2 = -1.863, \qquad s_3 = -0.0850 .
\end{equation*}
Without the integral the polynomial would be $s(s^2 + 7s + 10) = s(s+5)(s+2)$: the two poles of the mass–spring–damper and one at the origin. The integral gain has shifted them slightly and moved the third from the origin to $-0.0850$. The quasi-static estimate was $-K_i/K_p = -0.080$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the initial conditions.} At the switch the drone is at rest with $e = e_{ss} = 0.981$ and the integral starts from zero, so the forces still balance: $e(0) = 0.981$, $\dot e(0) = 0$, $\ddot e(0) = 0$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the solution.} $e(t) = c_1e^{s_1t} + c_2e^{s_2t} + c_3e^{s_3t}$. The three conditions are three linear equations for the constants, with the solution
\begin{equation*}
  c_3 = e_{ss}\,\frac{s_1 s_2}{(s_3 - s_1)(s_3 - s_2)} = 0.981 \cdot \frac{9.412}{4.967 \cdot 1.778} = 1.0455,
\end{equation*}
and, by the same formula with the indices exchanged, $c_1 = 0.0098$ and $c_2 = -0.0743$.

\textbf{Step 5 — evaluate.} The first two terms are gone after two seconds. After that $e(t) = 1.0455\,e^{-0.0850\,t}$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule

\midrule
error \SI{10}{s} after the switch & \SI{0.441}{m} & \SI{0.4469}{m} & \SI{0.4470}{m}\\
error \SI{20}{s} after the switch & \SI{0.198}{m} & \SI{0.1910}{m} & \SI{0.1911}{m}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Step 6 — the verdict.} The linear model reproduces the simulator to four digits: in this slow creep nothing saturates, drag is negligible and the motor lag does not matter. The shortcut is off by a few per cent, and it gave us the insight — a time constant of $K_p/K_i$ — that the three roots do not show at a glance. Use the shortcut to understand and the exact solution to be sure.
\end{example}

\section{Cure two: know the load}

The other cure is not to wait for an error at all. The weight is known — roughly.\didyouknow{An experienced driver feeds forward: the foot presses before the hill begins. A beginner waits until the speed drops. Both leave the rest to feedback.} So add it to the command directly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:droop-ff}
  T = \underbrace{\hat m\,g}_{\text{feedforward}} + \underbrace{K_p\,e + I - K_d\,\dot y}_{\text{feedback}} .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}{Feedforward}
A \term{feedforward} term is a part of the command computed from a model of the plant and from known inputs (the setpoint, a measured load), \emph{not} from the error. It acts immediately, without waiting for an error to appear, and it is exactly as good as the model.
\end{definition}

Now the feedback only has to correct the \emph{difference} between the real weight and the assumed one.

\begin{example}[Feedforward with a wrong mass]
\label{ex:droop-ff}
The feedforward uses $\hat m = \SI{0.9}{kg}$ for the \SI{1}{kg} drone; I is off, $K_p = 10$. Where does the drone settle?

\textbf{Step 1 — the equilibrium.} With \cref{eq:droop-ff} at rest: $0 = \hat m g + K_p e_{ss} - m g$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the error.} $e_{ss} = (m - \hat m)g/K_p = 0.1 \cdot 9.81/10 = \SI{0.098}{m}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — compare.} Without feedforward the droop was \SI{0.98}{m}; with a feedforward that is 10\,\% wrong it is \SI{0.098}{m}, ten times smaller.\tip{A feedforward that is 10\,\% wrong still removes 90\,\% of the problem. It need not be exact to be worth having.} The feedforward removes the part of the load it knows; feedback deals with the rest.
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}[Feedforward and feedback]
Feedforward acts on what you know, before any error exists. Feedback acts on what you do not know, after it has caused an error. Good controllers use both: the feedforward carries the bulk of the known load, the feedback (and especially its integral) mops up the model errors. Every flight controller in this book, up to the trajectory planners of Part~II, is built this way.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The engineer's view: which inputs leave an error}

This lesson found the steady-state error for one case, a constant load on a proportional loop. An engineer asks the question for the whole loop at once: which inputs does it follow exactly, and which leave an error? The method never changes. Assume that a steady regime exists, write down what is constant in it, and solve.

Two things act on the loop from outside, the setpoint $r$ and the load $d$, and each can be constant or changing. A constant setpoint with a known weight leaves no error even with P alone: the drone needs no extra force to stay where it is. The interesting cases are the other three.

\subsection{A setpoint that moves}
Let the setpoint climb at a constant rate, $r = r_0 + v_r t$, with the law of this lesson plus exact feedforward, $T = mg + K_p e - K_d\dot y$. In the steady regime the drone climbs at the same rate with a constant error: $\dot y = v_r$, $\ddot y = 0$. Newton's law then reads $0 = K_p e - K_d v_r$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:droop-ramp}
  e_{ss} = \frac{K_d\,v_r}{K_p} .
\end{equation}
The damper resists the climb, and P needs an error to overcome it.\didyouknow{A radar dish or a telescope that follows a satellite has this problem all the time: its setpoint is a ramp. Such loops are specified by their \emph{velocity error}.} For $v_r = \SI{0.5}{m/s}$ and the default gains that is $7 \cdot 0.5/10 = \SI{0.35}{m}$. The air adds its own resistance, $c_v v_r^2 = 0.25 \cdot 0.25 = \SI{0.06}{N}$, worth another $0.06/10 = \SI{0.006}{m}$.

If the derivative acts on the \emph{error} instead, the D term is $K_d(\dot r - \dot y)$, which vanishes when the drone keeps pace. Only the drag remains: \SI{0.006}{m}. The left panel of \cref{fig:droop-type} shows both, \SI{0.356}{m} and \SI{0.006}{m}, as predicted. \Lessonref{les:kick} will show what the derivative on the error costs in exchange.

\subsection{A load that changes}

\begin{example}[A drone that gets lighter]
\label{ex:droop-burn}
A spraying drone takes off at \SI{1.5}{kg}, which its controller knows, and then sprays \SI{50}{g} of liquid per second for ten seconds. The full PID is on. Where does it fly?

\textbf{Step 1 — the load.} The feedforward keeps pushing with $1.5\,g$ while the weight falls. The surplus is an upward load that grows at the rate $\dot d = 0.05 \cdot 9.81 = \SI{0.49}{N/s}$.\innumbers{A large crop-spraying drone carries 40 litres, close to half of its take-off weight, and empties the tank in a few minutes.}

\textbf{Step 2 — the steady regime.} Suppose the error settles at a constant $e$. Then P is constant, D is zero, and only the integral changes, at the rate $\dot I = K_i e$. To keep the forces in balance while the load ramps, the integral must ramp at the opposite rate: $K_i e = -\dot d$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:droop-burn}
  e_{ss} = -\frac{\dot d}{K_i} = -\frac{0.49}{0.8} = \SI{-0.61}{m} .
\end{equation}
The drone rides \SI{61}{cm} \emph{above} its setpoint for as long as it sprays. An integrator removes a constant load completely and a ramping load not at all: it only keeps the error from growing.

\textbf{Step 3 — the approach.} The quasi-static balance of Example~\ref{ex:droop-tau}, now with the load, is $K_p e + I + d = 0$. Differentiating: $K_p\dot e + K_i e + \dot d = 0$, a first-order equation with the solution $e(t) = e_{ss}\big(1 - e^{-t/\tau_I}\big)$, $\tau_I = K_p/K_i = \SI{12.5}{s}$.

\textbf{Step 4 — after ten seconds.} $e = -0.61\,(1 - e^{-0.8}) = \SI{-0.34}{m}$. The simulator shows \SI{-0.334}{m}.

\textbf{Step 5 — a stronger integral.} With $K_i = 3$: $e_{ss} = -0.49/3 = \SI{-0.16}{m}$, reached within the ten seconds because $\tau_I = \SI{3.3}{s}$. The simulator shows \SI{-0.159}{m} (\cref{fig:droop-type}, right).
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}[Count the integrators]
A loop follows without error exactly those inputs that its own integrators can produce. The drone integrates force twice on the way to position, so a constant setpoint costs nothing. One integrator in the controller, placed before the point where the load enters, holds a constant load; a ramping load would need two. Each integrator buys one more power of $t$, and each one costs stability, as \lessonref{les:integral} will show.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{In formal terms}
\enquote{The integral removes the droop} is true under three hypotheses, and each of them fails somewhere in this book.

\begin{theorem}[Integral action]
\label{thm:droop-integral}
Consider the altitude loop $m\ddot y = T - mg + d$ with a thrust limited to $0 \le T \le T_{max}$, the controller $T = \hat m g + K_p e + I - K_d\dot y$ with $\dot I = K_i e$, $K_i > 0$, and a constant setpoint $r$ and load $d$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item At every equilibrium $e = 0$ and $I^* = (m - \hat m)\,g - d$.
  \item An equilibrium exists if and only if the thrust it needs can be delivered: $0 \le mg - d \le T_{max}$.
  \item If that equilibrium is asymptotically stable, then $e(t) \to 0$ from every initial state in its region of attraction. For the loop away from its limits this holds if and only if $K_i < K_p K_d/m$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i)~At an equilibrium $\dot I = K_i e = 0$, so $e = 0$; with $\dot y = \ddot y = 0$ the plant gives $T^* = mg - d$ and the controller $T^* = \hat m g + I^*$. (ii)~$T^*$ must lie in the range of the motors. (iii)~The first claim is the definition of asymptotic stability. Near the equilibrium the loop is linear with the characteristic polynomial $m s^3 + K_d s^2 + K_p s + K_i$ (Example~\ref{ex:droop-exact}), and \cref{thm:spring-routh}\,(ii) with \cref{thm:spring-indirect} gives the condition.
\end{proof}

The three hypotheses are three ways to fail. The load may exceed what the motors can carry, and then there is no equilibrium to reach: \lessonref{les:windup}. The integral gain may be too large for stability: \lessonref{les:integral}. And the load may not be constant: Example~\ref{ex:droop-burn}. The theorem is the simplest case of the \emph{internal model principle}: to reject a class of disturbances completely, a controller must contain a generator of that class, and the generator of a constant is an integrator (Francis and Wonham, 1976).\didyouknow{The same principle in other clothes: to cancel a \SI{50}{Hz} hum completely, a controller must contain a \SI{50}{Hz} oscillator; to follow a ramp without error, two integrators.}

\begin{theorem}[Steady-state errors of the linear altitude loop]
\label{thm:droop-errors}
Let the loop be $m\ddot y = K_p e + I - K_d\dot y + d$ with $\dot I = K_i e$ (exact feedforward, no drag, no saturation), and let the setpoint and the load be $r(t) = r_0 + v_r t$ and $d(t) = d_0 + \dot d\,t$ with constants $v_r$, $d_0$, $\dot d$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Without the integral ($K_i = 0$, $I = 0$; $K_p, K_d > 0$): if $\dot d = 0$, then $e(t) \to (K_d v_r - d_0)/K_p$; if $\dot d \ne 0$, $|e(t)|$ grows without bound.
  \item With the integral ($0 < K_i < K_p K_d/m$): $e(t) \to -\dot d/K_i$, whatever $v_r$ and $d_0$ are.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
With $\dot y = v_r - \dot e$ and $\ddot y = -\ddot e$ the loop reads $m\ddot e + K_d\dot e + K_p e + I = K_d v_r - d$. Every solution of a linear equation is one particular solution plus a solution of the homogeneous equation, and the latter decays by \cref{thm:spring-routh}. (i)~If $\dot d = 0$ the right-hand side is constant and $e = (K_d v_r - d_0)/K_p$ is a particular solution; otherwise the particular solution is linear in $t$ with the slope $-\dot d/K_p$. (ii)~Differentiating once gives $m\dddot e + K_d\ddot e + K_p\dot e + K_i e = -\dot d$, with the constant particular solution $e = -\dot d/K_i$.
\end{proof}

The signs follow the convention of the book: $d$ is positive upwards, so a downward load $F$ is $d_0 = -F$ and part (i) returns the droop $F/K_p$ of this lesson.\pitfall{Sign check: $d$ counts upwards. A payload pulls down, so it is a \emph{negative} $d$, and it produces a \emph{positive} error — too low.} The table above lists the magnitudes.

The quasi-static shortcut of Example~\ref{ex:droop-tau} also has a name and a hypothesis. It is a \emph{singular perturbation} argument: a fast subsystem (the drone under P and D, poles at $-2$ and $-5$) is assumed to be at its equilibrium while a slow variable (the integral) moves. It is justified when the fast subsystem is asymptotically stable and the two time scales are far apart, and its error is of the order of their ratio (Khalil, 2002, ch.~11).\tip{Before a quasi-static shortcut, compare the time scales. Here \SI{12.5}{s} against \SI{0.5}{s}, a ratio of 25. Below about 5, do the full calculation.} Here the relative correction to the slow pole was $K_d K_i/K_p^2 = 0.056$.

\begin{lookahead}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item The table above is the beginning of a classification that engineers call the \emph{type} of a loop. Lesson~III.2 derives it for any loop from its transfer function, together with the Routh–Hurwitz test that limits how many integrators a loop can carry.
  \item The integral as one more state variable, with a gain chosen together with the others, is Lesson~II.3.
  \item Waiting \SI{12.5}{s} for an integral is slow. A \emph{disturbance observer} estimates the load directly from how the drone moves and cancels it within tenths of a second: Lessons~II.5 to~II.8.
  \item The derivative on the error removed the following error because it feeds the setpoint's velocity forward. Lesson~II.11 feeds forward the whole planned motion — velocity, acceleration and more — and cuts a tracking error from \SI{80}{cm} to \SI{2}{cm}.
  \item A rocket is the spraying drone of Example~\ref{ex:droop-burn} for its whole flight: its mass falls by the second. Lesson~IV.4 lands one. An aircraft's loads change with speed and altitude instead, and its gains follow them (Lesson~IV.13).
\end{itemize}
\end{lookahead}

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An equilibrium is found by setting all derivatives to zero.
  \item A proportional loop under a constant load $F$ settles with $e_{ss} = F/K_p$. Raising $K_p$ reduces the droop but cannot remove it.
  \item An integral can only rest at zero error, so it removes any constant droop — slowly, with time constant about $K_p/K_i$.
  \item Feedforward removes the known part of the load immediately; feedback handles what the model gets wrong.
  \item A setpoint that climbs at $v_r$ leaves $K_d v_r/K_p$ when D acts on the measurement. A load that ramps at $\dot d$ leaves $\dot d/K_i$ even with the integral on.
  \item A loop follows without error the inputs that its integrators can produce. Count them, and where they sit relative to the load.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{The grid that sags on purpose}
\photo{turbinehall}{A turbine hall of the 1920s. Every generator in a power grid runs a speed governor — with a deliberate droop.}
The electrical grid of a continent is one enormous machine turning in synchrony: every generator in Europe spins in step with a frequency of \SI{50}{Hz}. When you switch on a kettle, the load rises, the turbines slow down a little, and the frequency dips. Each power station answers with a speed governor that opens its steam or water valve.

Here is the surprise: those governors are \emph{proportional} controllers, and their droop is deliberate. A typical setting is 4–5\,\%: a generator increases its output by its full rated power if the frequency falls by 4–5\,\%. Why not an integral, which would restore \SI{50}{Hz} exactly? Because hundreds of generators share the load. If each of them integrated its own error, they would fight over it forever; with proportional droop every machine takes a share proportional to its size, and the sharing is stable.

The small remaining frequency error is then removed on a slower time scale, over tens of seconds to minutes, by a single central \emph{integral} loop, the secondary control, which asks selected plants to raise their setpoints. It is the structure of this lesson, spread over a continent: a fast proportional loop that accepts a droop, and a slow integral that pays it back.
\end{curiosity}
