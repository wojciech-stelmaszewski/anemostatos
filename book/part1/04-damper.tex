\section{The derivative term is a damper}\index{PID controller!derivative term}

With a constant setpoint, the error changes only because the drone moves: $\dot e = \dot r - \dot y = -\dot y$. The derivative term is therefore
\begin{equation}
  D = K_d\,\dot e = -K_d\,\dot y ,
\end{equation}
a force proportional to the vertical velocity and opposing it — exactly what a shock absorber does.\didyouknow{A car's shock absorber forces oil through small holes: the faster the wheel moves, the harder the oil resists. Without it the car would bounce on its springs for many seconds after every bump.} With exact feedforward, P and D together give the closed loop (with $x = y - r$, as in \lessonref{les:spring})
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damper-msd}
  m\,\ddot x + K_d\,\dot x + K_p\,x = 0 ,
\end{equation}
the equation of a \term{mass–spring–damper}.

\subsection{The damper removes energy}
Repeat the energy calculation of \lessonref{les:spring}: multiply \cref{eq:damper-msd} by $\dot x$ and use the chain rule,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2\Big) = \dot x\,(m\ddot x + K_p x) = -K_d\,\dot x^2 \;\le\; 0 .
\end{equation}
Whenever the drone moves, the D term removes energy at the rate $K_d\dot x^2$ — the power dissipated by a damper. The energy can only decrease, and it stops decreasing only when the drone stops moving; since a spring that is stretched does not let the drone stay still, the only final state is $x = 0$, $\dot x = 0$. The oscillation must die out.\tip{An energy argument answers \enquote{does it stop?} without solving anything. Try one before reaching for the characteristic equation.} The remaining questions are \emph{how fast} and \emph{in what manner}.

\section{Solving the damped equation}

We follow the recipe of \lessonref{les:spring} step by step.

\paragraph{The characteristic equation.} Substituting $x = e^{st}$ into \cref{eq:damper-msd} gives $m s^2 + K_d s + K_p = 0$. Divide by $m$ and introduce two new parameters:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damper-std}
  s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n\,s + \omega_n^2 = 0,\qquad
  \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{K_p}{m}},\qquad
  \zeta = \frac{K_d}{2\sqrt{K_p\,m}} .
\end{equation}
(Check: $2\zeta\omega_n = 2\cdot\frac{K_d}{2\sqrt{K_pm}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{K_p}{m}} = \frac{K_d}{m}$ and $\omega_n^2 = \frac{K_p}{m}$, as required.) This \emph{standard form} separates two questions. The \term{natural frequency} $\omega_n$ sets the time scale: how fast the loop wants to move. The \term{damping ratio} $\zeta$ (zeta) is dimensionless and sets the shape: how the motion approaches the target.\innumbers{Typical damping ratios: the suspension of a car 0.2 to 0.4; a door closer about 1; a wine glass, which rings for seconds, below 0.001.}

\paragraph{The roots.} The quadratic formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damper-poles}
  s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1} .
\end{equation}
The sign of $\zeta^2 - 1$ decides everything: three cases follow.\recall{$as^2 + bs + c = 0$ has the roots $\big({-b} \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}\,\big)/2a$. The sign under the root separates real roots from complex ones.}

\begin{definition}{The three kinds of damping}\index{damping!under-, over- and critical}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=3pt, font=\sffamily\small\bfseries]
  \item[Underdamped, $0<\zeta<1$.] The square root is imaginary: complex poles $-\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_d$ with the \emph{damped frequency} $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$. The motion is a decaying oscillation, $x(t) = e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}(A\cos\omega_d t + B\sin\omega_d t)$. The response overshoots.
  \item[Critically damped, $\zeta = 1$.] The root is zero: a double real pole at $-\omega_n$, and $x(t) = (A + Bt)\,e^{-\omega_n t}$. The fastest approach that never overshoots.
  \item[Overdamped, $\zeta > 1$.] Two distinct real poles, $x(t) = A e^{s_1 t} + B e^{s_2 t}$. The one closer to the origin makes the response slow: the damper is so strong that it resists the motion towards the target too.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

\widefig{zeta_family}{The damping ratio in two pictures. Left: the poles of \cref{eq:damper-std}, scaled by $\omega_n$. For $\zeta<1$ they lie on the unit circle (their distance from the origin is $\omega_n$) and move towards the real axis as $\zeta$ grows; at $\zeta=1$ they meet; beyond it they split along the axis and one of them crawls towards the origin. Right: the corresponding step responses. The grey band is the $\pm2\,\%$ tolerance used for the settling time.}{fig:zeta-family}

\Cref{fig:zeta-family} is worth a long look.\tip{Read the pole plane like a map. Further left: faster decay. Further up: faster oscillation. A small angle from the real axis: good damping. Poles at $45^\circ$ have $\zeta = 0.71$.} The pole plane is a map of behaviour: the height of a pole above the real axis is the frequency of oscillation, its distance to the left of the imaginary axis is the speed of decay, and — since $|s| = \omega_n$ for complex poles — the angle $\theta$ from the negative real axis tells the damping, $\cos\theta = \zeta$.

\begin{example}[The step response of the underdamped loop]
\label{ex:damper-under}
The setpoint jumps by \SI{1}{m} and $K_d = 3$ ($K_p = 10$, $m = 1$). Find the motion.

\textbf{Step 1 — the parameters.} $\omega_n = \sqrt{10} = \SI{3.162}{rad/s}$, $\zeta = 3/(2\sqrt{10}) = 0.474$, $\zeta\omega_n = K_d/(2m) = \SI{1.5}{s^{-1}}$, $\omega_d = 3.162\sqrt{1 - 0.225} = \SI{2.784}{rad/s}$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the general solution.} $x(t) = e^{-1.5t}(A\cos 2.784t + B\sin 2.784t)$.

\textbf{Step 3 — initial conditions.} Just after the step, $x(0) = -1$ (one metre below the new setpoint) and $\dot x(0) = 0$. The first gives $A = -1$. Differentiating, $\dot x(0) = -1.5A + 2.784B = 0$, so $B = 1.5A/2.784 = -0.539$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the result.} $x(t) = -e^{-1.5t}\big(\cos 2.784t + 0.539\sin 2.784t\big)$.

\textbf{Step 5 — interpret.} The oscillation has the period $2\pi/\omega_d = \SI{2.26}{s}$ — slightly longer than the undamped \SI{1.99}{s}\pitfall{A damped loop oscillates at $\omega_d$, not at $\omega_n$. If you read a period off a chart and compute a stiffness from it, correct for $\zeta$.} — and its envelope shrinks like $e^{-1.5t}$: to half in $\ln 2/1.5 = \SI{0.46}{s}$.
\end{example}

\section{Reading a step response}

To compare controllers we need numbers, not adjectives. The simulator measures four of them after every setpoint step, and shows them in the \ui{step metrics} table.

\begin{definition}{Step-response metrics}\index{step response!metrics}
For a step of the setpoint from $y_0$ to $y_1$:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=2pt, font=\sffamily\small\bfseries]
  \item[Rise time $t_r$] from 10\,\% to 90\,\% of the step.
  \item[Overshoot $M_p$] the peak beyond $y_1$, in \% of the step size.
  \item[Settling time $t_s$] the time after which the output stays within $\pm2\,\%$ of the step around $y_1$.
  \item[Steady-state error $e_{ss}$] what is left once everything has settled.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

For the second-order loop these metrics follow from the solution. We derive the two most used ones.

\begin{example}[Deriving the overshoot formula]\index{overshoot}
\label{ex:damper-mp}
Show that the overshoot of an underdamped second-order loop after a unit step is $M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$.

\textbf{Step 1 — the unit step response.} Repeating Example~\ref{ex:damper-under} with symbols ($x(0) = -1$, $\dot x(0) = 0$) gives, for $y = 1 + x$,
\begin{equation*}
  y(t) = 1 - e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\Big(\cos\omega_d t + \tfrac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_d t\Big).
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 2 — the velocity.} Differentiating and simplifying (the cosine terms cancel) leaves $\dot y(t) = \frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\,e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin\omega_d t$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the peak.} The first maximum is where the velocity first returns to zero after $t = 0$: $\sin\omega_d t = 0$, i.e.\ $t_p = \pi/\omega_d$.

\textbf{Step 4 — evaluate.} At $t_p$: $\cos\pi = -1$, $\sin\pi = 0$, so $y(t_p) = 1 + e^{-\zeta\omega_n\pi/\omega_d} = 1 + e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$.

\textbf{Step 5 — the result.} The overshoot is the part above 1:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damper-mp}
  M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} .
\end{equation}
It depends on $\zeta$ only.\innumbers{Worth knowing by heart: $\zeta = 0.5$ gives 16\,\% overshoot, $\zeta = 0.6$ about 10\,\%, $\zeta = 0.7$ about 5\,\%.} For $\zeta = 0.474$: $M_p = e^{-\pi\cdot0.474/0.881} = e^{-1.69} = 0.184$, i.e.\ 18.4\,\%, reached at $t_p = \pi/2.784 = \SI{1.13}{s}$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{The settling time.} The oscillation in Step 1 is multiplied by the envelope $e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}$ (times a factor close to one). The response is inside the 2\,\% band once the envelope has fallen below 0.02, i.e.\ when $\zeta\omega_n t = \ln 50 \approx 3.9$. Rounding up gives the rule\index{settling time}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damper-ts}
  t_s \approx \frac{4}{\zeta\,\omega_n}.
\end{equation}
For $K_d = 3$: $t_s \approx 4/1.5 = \SI{2.7}{s}$.\tip{The \enquote{4} is $\ln 50 = 3.9$ rounded up. For a band of 5\,\% use $\ln 20 = 3$; for 1\,\%, $\ln 100 = 4.6$.}

\begin{example}[The critically damped loop]
\label{ex:damper-crit}
Find the $K_d$ that gives $\zeta = 1$, and the settling time of the resulting step response ($K_p = 10$, $m = 1$).

\textbf{Step 1 — the gain.} $\zeta = 1$ means $K_d = 2\sqrt{K_p m} = 2\sqrt{10} = 6.32$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the response.} With the double pole at $-\omega_n$, the general solution $x = (A + Bt)e^{-\omega_n t}$ with $x(0) = -1$, $\dot x(0) = 0$ gives $A = -1$, $B = -\omega_n$, so
$y(t) = 1 - (1 + \omega_n t)\,e^{-\omega_n t}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — settling.} We need $(1 + \omega_n t)e^{-\omega_n t} = 0.02$. With $z = \omega_n t$, solve $(1+z)e^{-z} = 0.02$ by iteration,\tip{An equation like this has no closed solution. Rewrite it as $z = g(z)$ and iterate. When $g$ changes slowly, a few rounds are enough.} $z \leftarrow \ln\big(50(1+z)\big)$: starting from $z = 4$ gives $5.52$, then $5.77$, $5.81$, $5.83$ — converged: $z = 5.83$.

\textbf{Step 4 — numbers.} $t_s = 5.83/3.162 = \SI{1.84}{s}$. The rise time (solving $y = 0.1$ and $y = 0.9$ the same way: $z = 0.53$ and $3.89$) is $(3.89 - 0.53)/3.162 = \SI{1.06}{s}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The overdamped loop]
\label{ex:damper-over}
With $K_d = 15$ ($\zeta = 2.37$), where are the poles, and why is the response slow?

\textbf{Step 1 — the poles.} From \cref{eq:damper-poles}:
\begin{gather*}
  s = -2.37\cdot3.162 \pm 3.162\sqrt{2.37^2 - 1} = -7.50 \pm 6.80,\\
  \text{i.e.}\quad s_1 = -0.70 \quad\text{and}\quad s_2 = -14.3 .
\end{gather*}

\textbf{Step 2 — who dominates.} Both modes decay, but $e^{-14.3t}$ is gone within \SI{0.3}{s}, while $e^{-0.70t}$ has a time constant of \SI{1.43}{s}. After the first fraction of a second, only the slow pole matters: it is the \term{dominant pole}.

\textbf{Step 3 — settling.} The response enters the 2\,\% band when $e^{-0.70t} \approx 0.02$: $t \approx \ln 50/0.70 = \SI{5.6}{s}$ — three times slower than the critically damped loop, with a damper more than twice as strong.\pitfall{More damping is not more speed. Past $\zeta = 1$ every extra bit of $K_d$ makes the loop slower.}
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}[Critical damping]
For a given stiffness the fastest response without overshoot is the critically damped one, $\zeta = 1$, i.e.\ $K_d = 2\sqrt{K_p m}$. For the default $K_p = 10$ that is $K_d \approx 6.3$. Many practical tunes aim slightly below, $\zeta \approx 0.7$–$0.9$: a few percent of overshoot buys a noticeably faster rise.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{example}[Designing for a given overshoot]
\label{ex:damper-design}
Which $K_d$ gives 5\,\% overshoot with $K_p = 10$, $m = 1$? How long does it take to settle?

\textbf{Step 1 — invert \cref{eq:damper-mp}.} Taking logarithms, $\ln M_p = -\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$. With $L = -\ln 0.05 = 3.00$ this becomes $L^2(1 - \zeta^2) = \pi^2\zeta^2$, so
\begin{equation*}
  \zeta = \frac{L}{\sqrt{\pi^2 + L^2}} = \frac{3.00}{\sqrt{9.87 + 9.00}} = 0.69 .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 2 — the gain.} $K_d = 2\zeta\sqrt{K_pm} = 2\cdot0.69\cdot3.162 = 4.36$.

\textbf{Step 3 — settling.} $t_s \approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n) = 4/(0.69\cdot3.162) = \SI{1.83}{s}$ — about the same as critical damping, but with a faster rise.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[From a specification to gains — and what the formulas do not see]
\label{ex:damper-spec}
A setpoint step must settle within \SI{1}{s} with at most 5\,\% overshoot. Find $K_p$ and $K_d$, check what they ask of the motors, and fly the design.

\textbf{Step 1 — the damping, from the overshoot.} Example~\ref{ex:damper-design}: $\zeta = 0.69$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the speed, from the settling time.} \Cref{eq:damper-ts} with $t_s \le \SI{1}{s}$: $\omega_n \ge 4/(0.69 \cdot 1) = \SI{5.80}{rad/s}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the gains.} Comparing \cref{eq:damper-std} with the wanted $\omega_n$ and $\zeta$:
\begin{equation*}
  K_p = m\,\omega_n^2 = \SI{33.6}{N/m}, \qquad K_d = 2\zeta\omega_n m = \SI{8.0}{N.s/m}.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 4 — what the motors must do.} At the instant of a step of height $h$ the drone is still at rest, so the command is $mg + K_p h$.\tip{Always ask what a design demands of the actuators at its worst moment. For a step that is the very first instant, when P sees the whole error.} The motors deliver at most \SI{24.4}{N}, so the design stays linear only for
\begin{equation*}
  h \le \frac{24.4 - 9.81}{33.6} = \SI{0.43}{m}.
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 5 — fly it} (\cref{fig:damper-spec}). A step of \SI{0.4}{m}: the command peaks at \SI{23.2}{N}, the overshoot is 3.3\,\% and the response settles in \SI{0.83}{s}. A step of \SI{1}{m}: the command asks for \SI{43}{N}, the thrust is clipped at the limit for \SI{0.17}{s}, the rise takes \SI{0.37}{s} instead of \SI{0.32}{s}, the overshoot is 2.3\,\% and the settling time \SI{0.86}{s}.

\textbf{Step 6 — read the result.} Both steps meet the specification. The clipped one does so by luck of the numbers, not by design: its shape is no longer the one the formulas describe, and nothing in Steps 1–3 predicted it. A linear design comes with a range of validity, here \SI{0.43}{m}. Inside it the formulas are a guarantee. Outside it the design must be flown — and once an integral is in the loop, saturation is far less forgiving (\lessonref{les:windup}).
\end{example}

\section{The engineer's view: pole placement and Lyapunov}\index{pole placement}

\subsection{The state matrix and the path of its poles}
In state form the loop \eqref{eq:damper-msd} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:damper-state}
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \dot x \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -K_p/m & -K_d/m \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \dot x \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \det(s\Id - A) = s^2 + \frac{K_d}{m}\,s + \frac{K_p}{m} .
\end{equation}
The two gains are the two entries of the bottom row: each gain multiplies one state variable. A P–D controller on this plant is \emph{state feedback}, a name that Part~II takes literally.

Look again at the left panel of \cref{fig:zeta-family}, this time as a journey. As $K_d$ grows from zero, the eigenvalues leave $\pm j\omega_n$, travel along the circle of radius $\omega_n$, meet at $-\omega_n$ and separate along the real axis. A drawing of where the poles go as one gain changes is called a \term{root locus}.\didyouknow{Walter Evans invented the root locus\index{Evans, Walter} in 1948 while designing aircraft autopilots. He also sold a plastic tool for drawing the curves by hand, the Spirule.} Engineers read it as we just did: every point of the path is a behaviour, and tuning is choosing a point.

\subsection{Pole placement}
Example~\ref{ex:damper-spec} ran the argument backwards: from the wanted behaviour to the pole positions to the gains.

\begin{result}[Placing the poles of the altitude loop]
To put the closed-loop poles at $-\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$, choose
\begin{equation*}
  K_p = m\,\omega_n^2, \qquad K_d = 2\,\zeta\,\omega_n\,m .
\end{equation*}
Two gains place two poles anywhere in the plane. A specification on overshoot is a wedge around the negative real axis ($\zeta$ large enough), one on settling time a half-plane to the left of a vertical line ($\zeta\omega_n$ large enough). The poles must lie in both.
\end{result}

Anywhere — for the plant of \cref{eq:meet-plant}. The real drone objects twice. Its motors saturate, which limits how large a step a fast design can take (Example~\ref{ex:damper-spec}). And its motors lag and its controller samples, which limits how far left the poles can go at all; that limit is the subject of \lessonref{les:slow} and \lessonref{les:edge}. The formulas say where you \emph{may} put the poles.\pitfall{Pole placement never says no. Ask for poles at $-100$ and it answers $K_p = 10\,000$. Whether the motors can deliver it is not part of the formula.} The actuators say where you \emph{can}.

\subsection{Energy again: a first Lyapunov argument}
Return to the energy calculation at the start of this lesson. It proved that the oscillation dies out, and it never solved the differential equation. It used three facts:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a function of the state, $V = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2$, that is positive everywhere except at the target, where it is zero;
  \item along every motion $V$ does not increase: $\dot V = -K_d\dot x^2 \le 0$;
  \item $\dot V$ is zero only where $\dot x = 0$, and the system cannot stay at such a point unless $x = 0$ too, because the spring would set it moving.
\end{enumerate}
A function with the first two properties is called a \term{Lyapunov function}, after Aleksandr Lyapunov\index{Lyapunov, Aleksandr}, who introduced the method in 1892.\didyouknow{Lyapunov's thesis of 1892 was little read outside Russia for half a century. Western control engineers discovered it around 1960, and it has been the main tool of nonlinear control ever since.} The third step is known as LaSalle's invariance principle\index{LaSalle's invariance principle}; both are stated exactly below. Together they prove stability for systems whose equations cannot be solved at all: springs that are not linear, motors that saturate, a rigid body tumbling in three dimensions.

The right panel of \cref{fig:damper-predict} shows $V$ measured in flight. It falls in steps: steeply while the drone moves fast, and not at all at the turning points, where $\dot x = 0$ — exactly what $\dot V = -K_d\dot x^2$ says. The measured curve lies a little below the formula, because the air removes some energy too. And in four hundred samples it rises five times, by less than \SI{0.00004}{J} out of five: small enough to be the motor lag and the sampling, which the ideal loop does not have.

\subsection{In formal terms}
The three facts of the energy argument are the hypotheses of two theorems.

\begin{definition}{Positive definite functions and their derivative along solutions}
A continuously differentiable function $V : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is \term{positive definite} if $V(0) = 0$ and $V(\vx) > 0$ for $\vx \ne 0$. It is \term{radially unbounded} if $V(\vx) \to \infty$ as $\|\vx\| \to \infty$. Its \term{derivative along the solutions} of $\dot{\vx} = f(\vx)$ is $\dot V(\vx) = \nabla V(\vx) \cdot f(\vx)$, which can be computed without solving the equation.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Lyapunov's direct method]
\label{thm:damper-lyapunov}
Let $\vx = 0$ be an equilibrium of $\dot{\vx} = f(\vx)$ with $f$ locally Lipschitz, and let $V$ be positive definite on a neighbourhood of the origin. If $\dot V(\vx) \le 0$ there, the origin is stable. If $\dot V(\vx) < 0$ for all $\vx \ne 0$ there, it is asymptotically stable. (Khalil, 2002, ch.~4.)
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[The invariance principle]
\label{thm:damper-lasalle}
Let $V$ be positive definite and radially unbounded with $\dot V(\vx) \le 0$ on all of $\mathbb{R}^n$. If the only solution that stays in the set $\{\vx : \dot V(\vx) = 0\}$ for all time is $\vx(t) = 0$, then the origin is globally asymptotically stable. (Barbashin and Krasovskii; LaSalle. Khalil, 2002, ch.~4.)
\end{theorem}

\begin{result}[The P–D loop is globally asymptotically stable]
For every $m > 0$, $K_p > 0$ and $K_d > 0$ the equilibrium $x = 0$, $\dot x = 0$ of \cref{eq:damper-msd} is globally asymptotically stable.
\end{result}
\begin{proof}
$V = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2$ is positive definite and radially unbounded, and $\dot V = -K_d\dot x^2 \le 0$. This is zero on the whole line $\dot x = 0$ and not only at the origin, so \cref{thm:damper-lyapunov} gives stability and no more. A solution that stays on that line has $\dot x = 0$ for all time, hence $\ddot x = 0$, and \cref{eq:damper-msd} then gives $K_p x = 0$. \Cref{thm:damper-lasalle} applies.
\end{proof}

Nothing in this proof used the fact that the spring and the damper are linear.\tip{Looking for a Lyapunov function? Try the energy first. For mechanical systems it works surprisingly often.} It survives the air drag, which only makes $\dot V = -K_d\dot x^2 - c_v|\dot x|^3$ more negative, and a spring that stiffens (an exercise below). It does not survive the motor lag: then the state has a third variable and this $V$ need not decrease at every instant, which is consistent with the five small rises in \cref{fig:damper-predict}.

\begin{theorem}[Step response of the underdamped loop]
\label{thm:damper-step}
Let $\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot x + \omega_n^2 x = 0$ with $0 < \zeta < 1$, $x(0) = -1$, $\dot x(0) = 0$, and let $\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ and $\varphi = \arccos\zeta$. Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $x(t) = -\dfrac{e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\,\sin(\omega_d t + \varphi)$;
  \item the largest overshoot is $M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}$, reached at $t_p = \pi/\omega_d$;
  \item $|x(t)| \le \delta$ for all $t \ge \dfrac{1}{\zeta\omega_n}\Big(\ln\dfrac{1}{\delta} + \dfrac12\ln\dfrac{1}{1 - \zeta^2}\Big)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i)~The solution of Example~\ref{ex:damper-mp} contains $\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}\sin\omega_d t$. Since $\cos\varphi = \zeta$ and $\sin\varphi = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$, this equals $\sin(\omega_d t + \varphi)/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$. (ii)~Example~\ref{ex:damper-mp}; later peaks are smaller because the envelope decays. (iii)~$|\sin| \le 1$ in (i), solved for $t$.
\end{proof}

The rule $t_s \approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ of \cref{eq:damper-ts} is part (iii) with $\delta = 0.02$ and the second term dropped. Part (iii) is a guarantee and the rule an estimate: for $K_d = 3$ the guarantee is $(3.91 + 0.13)/1.5 = \SI{2.69}{s}$, and the simulator settles in \SI{2.5}{s}.

\begin{theorem}[Pole placement]
\label{thm:damper-placement}
Let $\dot{\vx} = A\vx + B u$ have one input and $n$ states, with the feedback $u = -K\vx$. A gain $K$ that gives $\det(s\Id - A + BK)$ any chosen real polynomial of degree $n$ with leading coefficient one exists if and only if the $n \times n$ matrix $\big(B \;\; AB \;\; \cdots \;\; A^{n-1}B\big)$ has rank $n$. The system is then called \term{controllable}. (Wonham, 1967; Kailath, 1980.)
\end{theorem}

For the drone, with $A$ and $B$ of \cref{eq:meet-lti},
\begin{equation*}
  \big(B \;\; AB\big) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1/m \\ 1/m & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
has rank two, and $K = (K_p,\ K_d)$ gives $\det(s\Id - A + BK) = s^2 + (K_d/m)\,s + K_p/m$: the gains of the result above, and the only ones. Controllability is a property of the vehicle, not of the controller.\didyouknow{Kálmán defined controllability in 1960. Before that, engineers had simply assumed that a plant with an actuator could be steered wherever they liked.} A drone that loses a rotor loses rank, and no gain can replace it (Lessons~III.9 and~III.27).

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item With a constant setpoint the D term is $-K_d\dot y$: a damper. It removes energy at the rate $K_d\dot y^2$.
  \item P and D make a mass–spring–damper with $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_p/m}$ and $\zeta = K_d/(2\sqrt{K_p m})$; its poles are $-\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}$.
  \item $\zeta<1$ overshoots by $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$, $\zeta=1$ is the fastest without overshoot, $\zeta>1$ crawls on its dominant pole.
  \item Settling time $\approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ for $\zeta<1$, $5.83/\omega_n$ for $\zeta=1$.
  \item To place the poles: $K_p = m\omega_n^2$, $K_d = 2\zeta\omega_n m$. The design is linear only while $mg + K_p h$ stays below the motor limit.
  \item $V = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2$ with $\dot V = -K_d\dot x^2 \le 0$ proves stability without solving anything: a Lyapunov function.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{A 660-tonne pendulum}
\photo{taipei}{The tuned mass damper of Taipei 101: a steel sphere of 660 tonnes hanging between the 87th and the 92nd floor.}
Taipei 101 was the tallest building in the world when it opened in 2004, and it stands in a place that is hit by typhoons and earthquakes. A tall building is a spring with very little damping: steel and concrete dissipate almost no energy, so wind gusts can set it swaying in a way that makes the people at the top seasick.

The engineers hung a giant pendulum — a sphere of welded steel plates weighing 660 tonnes — near the top of the tower, tuned so that its natural frequency matches the building's. When the tower sways, the pendulum lags behind and pushes against the motion; eight large hydraulic dampers connected to it turn that relative motion into heat. In our language, the pendulum and its dampers add a large $K_d$ to a structure that had almost none, and they cut the swaying by up to 40\,\% in strong winds. During Typhoon Soudelor in 2015 the sphere swung by a metre.

Your car contains four much smaller dampers doing the same job. Car suspensions are usually tuned to $\zeta \approx 0.2$–$0.4$ — well below critical — because a completely non-oscillating ride feels harsh. Comfort, it turns out, is a choice of damping ratio.
\end{curiosity}

\exercise[1] The drone carries a payload so that $m = \SI{1.5}{kg}$, with $K_p = 10$. Which $K_d$ is critical? If $K_d$ stays at 6.3, what are $\zeta$ and the overshoot?
\answer{$K_d = 2\sqrt{15} = 7.75$. With $K_d = 6.3$: $\zeta = 0.81$, $M_p \approx 1.3\,\%$.}
\exercise[1] Find $K_p$ and $K_d$ for $m = \SI{1}{kg}$ such that $\zeta = 0.7$ and $t_s \approx \SI{1}{s}$.
\answer{$\zeta\omega_n = 4$, $\omega_n = 5.71$, $K_p = 32.7$, $K_d = 8.0$.}
\exercise[1] For $K_d = 3$, compute the time of the first peak and the time of the first undershoot below the setpoint after it.
\answer{$t_p = \pi/\omega_d = \SI{1.13}{s}$; the next extremum at $2\pi/\omega_d = \SI{2.26}{s}$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} Show that for $\zeta > 1$ the slow pole satisfies $s \approx -\omega_n/(2\zeta)$ when $\zeta \gg 1$. Interpret: the drone then moves as if only the damper and the spring existed, with no mass.
\answer{$-\zeta\omega_n + \zeta\omega_n\sqrt{1-1/\zeta^2} \approx -\omega_n/(2\zeta) = -K_p/K_d$: the pole of $K_d\dot x + K_p x = 0$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} Using the solution of Example~\ref{ex:damper-mp}, show that the ratio of two successive peaks of the oscillation (measured from the setpoint) is $e^{-2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$. How can you use this to measure $\zeta$ from a recorded response?
\answer{Successive maxima are $2\pi/\omega_d$ apart, so the envelope ratio is $e^{-\zeta\omega_n\cdot2\pi/\omega_d}$. Measuring the ratio $\rho$ of two peaks gives $\delta = -\ln\rho$ (logarithmic decrement) and $\zeta = \delta/\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}$.}
\exercise[3]\exkind{derive} The pole angle: show that complex poles of \cref{eq:damper-std} make the angle $\theta$ with the negative real axis such that $\cos\theta = \zeta$. What region of the $s$-plane guarantees overshoot below 5\,\% and settling within \SI{2}{s}?
\answer{$|s| = \omega_n$, $\operatorname{Re}s = -\zeta\omega_n$, so $\cos\theta = \zeta$. Overshoot $\le 5\,\%$ needs $\zeta \ge 0.69$ ($\theta \le 46^\circ$); $t_s \le 2$ needs $\operatorname{Re}s \le -2$. The intersection of the wedge and the half-plane.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} A spring that stiffens as it stretches pulls with the force $-K_p x - k_3 x^3$, $k_3 > 0$. The loop $m\ddot x + K_d\dot x + K_p x + k_3 x^3 = 0$ has no solution in elementary functions. Find a function $V(x, \dot x)$ with $\dot V = -K_d\dot x^2$ and conclude that the drone still comes to rest at the setpoint.
\answer{$V = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 K_p x^2 + \tfrac14 k_3 x^4$: positive except at the origin, and $\dot V = \dot x(m\ddot x + K_p x + k_3x^3) = -K_d\dot x^2$. $\dot V = 0$ only at $\dot x = 0$, where the spring force is non-zero unless $x = 0$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} Give the satellite of Example~\ref{ex:spring-sat} ($J = \SI{12}{kg.m^2}$, $K_p = \SI{0.3}{N.m/rad}$) a rate feedback $-K_d\dot\theta$. Which $K_d$ gives $\zeta = 0.7$, and how long does a pointing error then take to settle?
\answer{$K_d = 2\zeta\sqrt{K_pJ} = 1.4\sqrt{3.6} = \SI{2.66}{N.m.s/rad}$; $t_s \approx 4/(\zeta\omega_n) = 4/(0.7\cdot0.158) = \SI{36}{s}$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{fly} Fly Example~\ref{ex:damper-spec}. In Lesson~I.4 set $K_p = 33.6$ and $K_d = 8$. The square wave makes steps of \SI{1}{m}: read the overshoot and the settling time from the step metrics and watch the thrust chart for the red shading of saturation. Then set the amplitude of the square wave to \SI{0.2}{m}, which makes steps of \SI{0.4}{m}, and compare. Finally find, by trial, the largest step that shows no red at all, and compare it with Step~4 of the example.
\answer{About 2\,\% and \SI{0.86}{s} for the large step, 3\,\% and \SI{0.83}{s} for the small one. The red shading first appears at a step of about \SI{0.43}{m}, as Step~4 predicts.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{code} In \src{src/engine/metrics.ts} the function \texttt{analyzeLastStep} has a parameter \texttt{band} with the default 0.02. Use the envelope argument behind \cref{eq:damper-ts} to predict the settling time of the $K_d = 3$ loop for a band of 5\,\%. Change the default, fly the lesson and compare. Then run \texttt{make test}: which expectations depended on the 2\,\% definition?

\exercise[3]\exkind{derive} The function $V$ of this lesson has a derivative that is only negative \emph{semi}definite, which is why \cref{thm:damper-lasalle} was needed. Show that $W = V + \beta\,m\,x\dot x$ is positive definite for $0 < \beta < \sqrt{K_p/m}$ and that $\dot W$ is negative definite for $0 < \beta < 4K_pK_d/(K_d^2 + 4mK_p)$, so that \cref{thm:damper-lyapunov} alone proves asymptotic stability. Evaluate both bounds for the default gains.
\answer{$W$ is a quadratic form with the matrix $\tfrac12\begin{psmallmatrix}K_p & \beta m\\ \beta m & m\end{psmallmatrix}$, positive definite when $\beta^2m^2 < mK_p$. $\dot W = -(K_d - \beta m)\dot x^2 - \beta K_d x\dot x - \beta K_p x^2$, negative definite when $4(K_d - \beta m)\beta K_p > \beta^2K_d^2$. For $K_p = 10$, $K_d = 7$, $m = 1$: $\beta < 3.16$ and $\beta < 280/89 = 3.15$.}
