\section{How much a derivative amplifies}

Until now the sensor was perfect. From here on the measurement is the truth plus an error that changes at random from one reading to the next.

\begin{definition}{Measurement noise}
The measured altitude is $y_k = y_k^{true} + n_k$. The \term{noise} $n_k$ is modelled as a sequence of random numbers with mean zero and standard deviation $\sigma$, each independent of all the others. Such a sequence is called \term{white noise}. In this lesson it is Gaussian with $\sigma = \SI{1}{cm}$.
\end{definition}

\enquote{Independent} is the important word: knowing one reading's error tells nothing about the next. That is what makes noise fast — it can change by its full size from one sample to the next — and it is what a derivative picks up.

\begin{example}[The noise in the raw D term]
\label{ex:noise-raw}
How much does \SI{1}{cm} of altimeter noise disturb the D term, with $K_d = 7$, $\Delta t = \SI{4}{ms}$ and no filter?

\textbf{Step 1 — the derivative of the measurement.}
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{y_k - y_{k-1}}{\Delta t} = \underbrace{\frac{y^{true}_k - y^{true}_{k-1}}{\Delta t}}_{\text{the drone's velocity}} + \underbrace{\frac{n_k - n_{k-1}}{\Delta t}}_{\text{noise}} .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 2 — the variance of a difference.} For independent random numbers variances add, also under subtraction: $\operatorname{Var}(n_k - n_{k-1}) = \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 = 2\sigma^2$. The difference has the standard deviation $\sqrt2\,\sigma = \SI{1.41}{cm}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — divide by the sample time.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise-gain}
  \sigma_{\dot y} = \frac{\sqrt2\,\sigma}{\Delta t} = \frac{0.0141}{0.004} = \SI{3.5}{m/s}.
\end{equation}
The controller believes that the drone's speed jumps around by several metres per second, while it is actually hovering.

\textbf{Step 4 — times the gain.} $K_d\,\sigma_{\dot y} = 7 \cdot 3.54 = \SI{24.7}{N}$: two and a half times the weight of the drone, fluctuating 250 times per second.

\textbf{Step 5 — check.} The standard deviation of the D term in the simulator's run is \SI{23.9}{N} (\cref{fig:noise-filters}, left column: a violet hairball of $\pm\SI{50}{N}$).

\textbf{Step 6 — and the P term?} It multiplies the same noise by $K_p$ only: $10 \cdot 0.01 = \SI{0.1}{N}$. The integral adds noise up, and independent errors average out. Of the three terms only D is a problem.
\end{example}

Note the $1/\Delta t$ in \cref{eq:noise-gain}: a faster controller makes the noise in the derivative \emph{worse}. Sampling twice as often halves the time between readings and leaves their errors as large as before.

\subsection{The same thing, seen in frequency}
A sinusoid $\sin\omega t$ has the derivative $\omega\cos\omega t$: a differentiator multiplies the amplitude of each frequency by $\omega$. The drone's motion lives below a few hertz. White noise spreads its power evenly over all frequencies that the sampling can represent, up to half the sample rate, \SI{125}{Hz}.\aside{Sampling at the rate $f_s$ can only represent frequencies up to $f_s/2$, the \emph{Nyquist frequency}. What happens to anything faster is the subject of Lesson~III.20.} So the derivative amplifies most of the noise a hundred times more than it amplifies the signal (\cref{fig:noise-bode}).

\simfig{noise_bode}{The gain of a differentiator grows with frequency (red). A first-order low-pass filter caps it above the cutoff. The art is to put the cutoff above the frequencies of the motion (grey) and below those of the noise (red band).}{fig:noise-bode}

\section{The filtered derivative}

The standard cure is to pass the derivative through a \term{low-pass filter}, which lets slow changes through and averages fast ones away. Anemostatos uses a first-order filter with the cutoff frequency $f_c$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise-filter}
  D_k = \alpha\,D_{k-1} + (1 - \alpha)\,D^{raw}_k,\qquad
  \alpha = \frac{T_f}{T_f + \Delta t},\qquad T_f = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c}.
\end{equation}
Each new output is a weighted average of the previous output and the new input. It is the discrete form of the differential equation $T_f\dot D + D = D^{raw}$: the output follows the input with the time constant $T_f$. (Replace $\dot D$ by $(D_k - D_{k-1})/\Delta t$ and solve for $D_k$ to get \cref{eq:noise-filter}.)

\begin{example}[The noise that gets through the filter]
\label{ex:noise-filtered}
Compute the fluctuation of the D term with the default \SI{20}{Hz} filter.

\textbf{Step 1 — the filter constant.} $T_f = 1/(2\pi \cdot 20) = \SI{7.96}{ms}$, $\alpha = 7.96/11.96 = 0.666$.

\textbf{Step 2 — follow one noise sample.} Because everything is linear, we may ask what a single error $n_0 = 1$ (all others zero) does to the output, and add the effects of all samples afterwards. The raw derivative is $+1/\Delta t$ at sample 0 and $-1/\Delta t$ at sample 1. Through the filter:
\begin{equation*}
  h_0 = \frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta t}, \qquad h_1 = \alpha h_0 - \frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta t} = -\frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\Delta t}, \qquad h_k = \alpha\,h_{k-1} \ \ (k \ge 2).
\end{equation*}
So one bad reading produces a push, then a smaller pull that dies away geometrically.

\textbf{Step 3 — add up all the samples.} The output is $\sum_j h_j\,n_{k-j}$, a sum of independent terms, so its variance is $\sigma^2\sum_j h_j^2$:
\begin{align*}
  \sum_j h_j^2 &= \frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\Delta t^2}\Big(1 + (1-\alpha)^2\big(1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha^4 + \dots\big)\Big)\\
  &= \frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\Delta t^2}\Big(1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\Big) = \frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\Delta t^2}\cdot\frac{2}{1+\alpha},
\end{align*}
using the geometric series $1 + \alpha^2 + \dots = 1/(1-\alpha^2)$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the result.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise-filtered}
  \sigma_D = K_d\,\frac{\sigma}{\Delta t}\,(1-\alpha)\sqrt{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}} .
\end{equation}
For $\alpha = 0$ (no filter) this is \cref{eq:noise-gain} again.

\textbf{Step 5 — numbers.} $\sigma_D = 7 \cdot 2.5 \cdot 0.334 \cdot \sqrt{2/1.666} = 7 \cdot 2.5 \cdot 0.334 \cdot 1.096 = \SI{6.4}{N}$. The simulator: \SI{6.2}{N}.

\textbf{Step 6 — all the cutoffs} (\cref{fig:noise-predict}, left). For 50, 20, 10, 5, 2 and \SI{1}{Hz} the formula gives 11.5, 6.4, 3.7, 2.0, 0.85 and \SI{0.43}{N}; the simulator 11.1, 6.2, 3.6, 2.0, 0.83 and \SI{0.42}{N}.
\end{example}

For a slow filter ($T_f \gg \Delta t$) we have $1 - \alpha \approx \Delta t/T_f$ and the square root is close to one, so \cref{eq:noise-filtered} simplifies to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise-simple}
  \sigma_D \approx \frac{K_d\,\sigma}{T_f} = 2\pi f_c\,K_d\,\sigma .
\end{equation}
The noise in the D term is proportional to the cutoff frequency, and no longer depends on the sample rate: the filter, not the sampling, now decides how fast a change the derivative is allowed to see.

\section{A surprise: noise that moves the drone}

Look again at the unfiltered run in \cref{fig:noise-filters}: the drone does not merely jitter, it hovers well above the setpoint. Noise has zero mean. How can it push?

Through saturation. The command swings by tens of newtons around the hover thrust, but the motors deliver only between 0 and \SI{24.4}{N}. Hover is at \SI{9.81}{N}, closer to the lower limit than to the upper one: the downward swings are cut off after \SI{9.8}{N}, the upward ones only after \SI{14.6}{N}. More of the upward half survives.

\begin{example}[The bias of clipped noise]
\label{ex:noise-rect}
Predict where the drone hovers without a D filter.

\textbf{Step 1 — the command is a random number.} In each sample the command is $u = \mu + \text{noise}$, approximately Gaussian with the standard deviation $s = \SI{24.7}{N}$ (Example~\ref{ex:noise-raw}) around a mean $\mu$ set by the slow terms: $\mu = 9.81 + K_p e + I$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the mean of the clipped command.} For a Gaussian clipped to $[0, u_{max}]$, with $a = (0 - \mu)/s$, $b = (u_{max} - \mu)/s$, the normal distribution function $\Phi$ and its density $\varphi$:
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}[\sat(u)] = u_{max}\big(1 - \Phi(b)\big) + \mu\,\big(\Phi(b) - \Phi(a)\big) + s\,\big(\varphi(a) - \varphi(b)\big).
\end{equation*}
The three terms are: the samples clipped at the top, the samples passed unchanged, and a correction for the cut-off tails.

\textbf{Step 3 — at hover.} With $\mu = 9.81$: $a = -0.396$, $b = 0.590$; $\Phi(a) = 0.346$, $\Phi(b) = 0.722$, $\varphi(a) = 0.369$, $\varphi(b) = 0.335$. So 35\,\% of the commands are clipped at zero and 28\,\% at the top, and
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}[\sat(u)] = 24.4 \cdot 0.278 + 9.81 \cdot 0.376 + 24.7 \cdot 0.034 = 6.78 + 3.69 + 0.83 = \SI{11.3}{N}.
\end{equation*}
The motors deliver \SI{1.5}{N} more than the \SI{9.81}{N} that was meant. The drone accelerates upwards.

\textbf{Step 4 — the new balance.} The drone rises until the feedback has lowered $\mu$ enough for the mean thrust to equal the weight again. Solving $\mathbb{E}[\sat(u)] = 9.81$ for $\mu$ (by trial, or bisection) gives $\mu^* = \SI{5.81}{N}$: the slow terms must supply $K_p e + I = 5.81 - 9.81 = \SI{-4.0}{N}$.

\textbf{Step 5 — the altitude.} At first the integral is empty and P carries it alone: $e = -4.0/10 = \SI{-0.40}{m}$. The drone should hover \SI{40}{cm} high, and then sink back slowly as the integral takes over.

\textbf{Step 6 — check} (\cref{fig:noise-predict}, right). Six seconds into the run the drone is \SI{36}{cm} high; P and I together average \SI{-3.8}{N} over the run. The integral does take over, but more slowly than the $K_p/K_i = \SI{12.5}{s}$ of \lessonref{les:droop}: the default anti-windup freezes it whenever the command is clipped at zero with the error pushing further down (\lessonref{les:windup}), which is 40\,\% of the time.
\end{example}

A nonlinearity has turned symmetric noise into a constant force. The effect is called \term{rectification}, and it is common wherever noisy signals meet hard limits. It is one more reason to keep noise out of the actuators: with the default \SI{20}{Hz} filter only 7\,\% of the commands are clipped and the bias is \SI{0.7}{cm}.

\section{The price of filtering}

A filter is not free: it delays. For an input that changes slowly the first-order filter lags behind by about its time constant $T_f$: \SI{8}{ms} at \SI{20}{Hz}, \SI{32}{ms} at \SI{5}{Hz}, \SI{160}{ms} at \SI{1}{Hz}. The damper then reacts to the velocity the drone had a moment ago.

\begin{example}[What a slow filter does to the damping]
\label{ex:noise-delay}
The ideal P–D loop (exact feedforward, no motor lag) with the D term filtered: where are its poles for cutoffs of 20, 5 and \SI{1}{Hz}?

\textbf{Step 1 — the equations.} $m\ddot x = -K_p x + D$ with the filter $T_f\dot D + D = -K_d\dot x$.

\textbf{Step 2 — eliminate $D$.} From the first equation $D = m\ddot x + K_p x$. Insert it into the second:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise-cubic}
  T_f m\,\dddot x + m\,\ddot x + (K_d + K_p T_f)\,\dot x + K_p\,x = 0 .
\end{equation}
The filter has added a third pole. For $T_f = 0$ the equation is the mass–spring–damper of \lessonref{les:damper}.

\textbf{Step 3 — is it stable?} \Cref{thm:spring-routh}\,(ii): $a_2a_1 = m(K_d + K_pT_f)$ exceeds $a_3a_0 = T_f m K_p$ for every $T_f$, since $K_d > 0$. Filtering D alone never destabilises this loop.

\textbf{Step 4 — but it changes the poles.} Solving the cubic (as in Example~\ref{ex:integral-poles}) with $K_p = 10$, $K_d = 7$:
\begin{center}\small\sffamily
\begin{tabular}{rrl}
\toprule
cutoff & $T_f$ & poles\\
\midrule
no filter & 0 & $-2.00$, $-5.00$\\
\SI{20}{Hz} & \SI{8}{ms} & $-1.93$, $-5.50$, $-118$\\
\SI{5}{Hz} & \SI{32}{ms} & $-1.77$, $-8.32$, $-21.3$\\
\SI{1}{Hz} & \SI{159}{ms} & $-1.33$, $-2.48 \pm 6.42j$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Step 5 — read them.} At \SI{20}{Hz} the loop is unchanged: the new pole is far away and the old ones barely move. At \SI{1}{Hz} the dominant pole has moved from $-2$ to $-1.33$ — a slower approach — and a lightly damped pair has appeared ($\zeta = 0.36$, a ripple of about \SI{1}{Hz}). A slow filter has turned the damper into something closer to a second spring.

\textbf{Step 6 — check.} Flown on the lesson's square wave without noise, the settling time grows from \SI{2.32}{s} (\SI{20}{Hz}) to \SI{2.43}{s} (\SI{5}{Hz}) and \SI{2.97}{s} (\SI{1}{Hz}).
\end{example}

\begin{keyidea}[Noise against delay]
Every way of reducing noise — filtering, averaging, slower sampling — adds delay, and delay weakens the loop. Choosing a filter is choosing a point on that trade-off: the cutoff should sit above the frequencies you want to control and below the frequencies of the noise. When the two overlap, no filter can help, and you need a better sensor or a better estimator. Part II's Kalman filter (\lessonref{les:kalman}) is the systematic answer: it uses a model to separate signal from noise without paying the full price in delay.
\end{keyidea}

A useful rule: the filter time constant should be several times shorter than the time scale of the closed loop. The altitude loop has $\omega_n \approx \SI{3}{rad/s}$, a time scale of about \SI{0.3}{s}; a \SI{20}{Hz} filter ($T_f = \SI{8}{ms}$) is far below it and costs almost nothing, a \SI{1}{Hz} filter ($\SI{160}{ms}$) does not.

\begin{watchout}[Derivative on measurement does not help with noise]
\Lessonref{les:kick} moved the derivative from the error to the measurement. That removes the kick from setpoint steps, but not the noise: the noise is in the measurement.
\end{watchout}

\section{The engineer's view: every block is a filter}

\subsection{Frequency response}
\Cref{fig:noise-bode} answered a question that Lessons 2–7 never asked: what does a block do to a sinusoid? For linear blocks the answer is always of the same kind, and it is found by the method of \lessonref{les:spring} — try an exponential. Feed the filter $T_f\dot D + D = x$ with $x = e^{j\omega t}$ and look for a response $D = H\,e^{j\omega t}$. Substituting, $(j\omega T_f + 1)\,H = 1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise-H}
  H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega T_f}, \qquad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \omega^2T_f^2}}, \qquad \angle H = -\arctan(\omega T_f) .
\end{equation}
The real part of this statement is what we want: a sinusoid of frequency $\omega$ comes out as a sinusoid of the same frequency, scaled by $|H|$ and shifted in phase by $\angle H$. The complex number $H(j\omega)$ is the \term{frequency response} of the block. A differentiator has $H = j\omega$: gain $\omega$, phase $+90^\circ$. The two in series multiply: $j\omega/(1 + j\omega T_f)$, the curves of \cref{fig:noise-bode}.

At the frequency of the loop, $\omega = \SI{3.16}{rad/s}$, the filter's phase lag is $\arctan(3.16\,T_f)$: $1.4^\circ$ at \SI{20}{Hz}, $5.7^\circ$ at \SI{5}{Hz}, $26.7^\circ$ at \SI{1}{Hz}. A phase lag at the loop's own frequency is what costs damping; \lessonref{les:slow} turns this into a design rule.

\subsection{A noise budget}
With frequency responses, the trade-off of this lesson becomes a calculation that engineers do before they choose a sensor:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item how much fluctuation can the actuators tolerate (say, \SI{1}{N} for our motors);
  \item which cutoff does the loop need (from its time scale: here at least \SI{5}{Hz});
  \item by \cref{eq:noise-simple}, which sensor noise is then allowed: $\sigma \le \sigma_D/(2\pi f_cK_d) = 1/(2\pi \cdot 5 \cdot 7) = \SI{4.5}{mm}$.
\end{enumerate}
If the sensor is worse than that, no tuning will fix it.

\begin{example}[Vertical speed from satellite navigation]
\label{ex:noise-gps}
An aircraft's autopilot needs the vertical speed to within \SI{0.5}{m/s}. A satellite-navigation receiver reports the altitude ten times per second with an error of \SI{3}{m}. Can the vertical speed be obtained by differentiating it?

\textbf{Step 1 — the raw derivative.} \Cref{eq:noise-gain}: $\sqrt2 \cdot 3/0.1 = \SI{42}{m/s}$. Useless.

\textbf{Step 2 — the filter it would take.} \Cref{eq:noise-simple} without the gain: $\sigma/T_f \le 0.5$ needs $T_f \ge 3/0.5 = \SI{6}{s}$.

\textbf{Step 3 — what that costs.} A vertical-speed signal that is six seconds late cannot damp an altitude loop that should react within a few seconds.

\textbf{Step 4 — the way out.} The aircraft also carries an accelerometer, whose integral gives the vertical speed with very little noise but a slow drift. One sensor is good at high frequencies, the other at low ones. Combining them — the fast one through a high-pass filter, the slow one through a low-pass — gives both. That is a \emph{complementary filter}, and its optimal version is the Kalman filter of \lessonref{les:kalman}.
\end{example}

\subsection{In formal terms}

\begin{theorem}[White noise through a linear filter]
\label{thm:noise-variance}
Let $n_k$ be independent random variables with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$, and let $z_k = \sum_{j \ge 0} h_j\,n_{k-j}$ with $\sum_j h_j^2 < \infty$. Then $z_k$ has mean zero and variance
\begin{equation*}
  \operatorname{Var} z_k = \sigma^2\sum_{j \ge 0} h_j^2 .
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The mean is linear. For the variance, $\mathbb{E}[z_k^2] = \sum_i\sum_j h_ih_j\,\mathbb{E}[n_{k-i}n_{k-j}]$, and independence makes every term with $i \ne j$ vanish.
\end{proof}

The sequence $h_j$ is the response of the filter to a single unit sample, its \term{impulse response}; Example~\ref{ex:noise-filtered} computed it for the filtered derivative. The hypothesis is independence. Real sensor noise is often correlated from sample to sample, and then the cross terms do not vanish: a slowly wandering error (a bias, a drift) passes through a low-pass filter untouched.

\begin{theorem}[Frequency response]
\label{thm:noise-freq}
Let a block be described by $a_n\,y^{(n)} + \dots + a_1\dot y + a_0 y = b_m\,x^{(m)} + \dots + b_0 x$ with constant real coefficients, and let all roots of $a_ns^n + \dots + a_0$ have negative real parts. Then for the input $x = \sin\omega t$ the output tends to
\begin{equation*}
  y(t) = |H(j\omega)|\,\sin\big(\omega t + \angle H(j\omega)\big), \qquad H(s) = \frac{b_ms^m + \dots + b_0}{a_ns^n + \dots + a_0} .
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$y = H(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}$ solves the equation for $x = e^{j\omega t}$, by substitution; its imaginary part solves it for $\sin\omega t$. Every other solution differs from it by a solution of the homogeneous equation, which decays (\cref{thm:spring-linear}).
\end{proof}

The function $H(s)$ is the \term{transfer function} of the block. We shall use it more and more: in \lessonref{les:slow} for the whole loop, and throughout Part~III.

\begin{theorem}[Sampling]
\label{thm:noise-sampling}
A signal that contains no frequencies at or above $f_s/2$ is determined exactly by its samples taken at the rate $f_s$. A component at a frequency $f > f_s/2$ appears in the samples as a component at a frequency below $f_s/2$, from which it cannot be told apart. (Shannon, 1949; Oppenheim and Schafer, 2010, ch.~4.)
\end{theorem}

The second sentence is called \emph{aliasing}, and it limits what any digital filter can do: noise that has already folded down into the band of the motion cannot be filtered out afterwards. It must be removed before sampling, by an analogue filter in the sensor.

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item White noise of size $\sigma$ reaches an unfiltered D term as $\sqrt2\,K_d\sigma/\Delta t$; faster sampling makes it worse.
  \item A first-order filter with time constant $T_f = 1/(2\pi f_c)$ reduces it to about $K_d\sigma/T_f$, at the price of a lag of about $T_f$.
  \item Keep $T_f$ several times shorter than the loop's time scale; if the noise then is still too large, the sensor is not good enough.
  \item Noise plus saturation makes a bias (rectification).
  \item A linear block turns a sinusoid into a sinusoid: gain $|H(j\omega)|$, phase $\angle H(j\omega)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{The sensor in every drone}
\sidephoto{mpu6050}{45mm}{An MPU-6050: a three-axis gyroscope and a three-axis accelerometer in a package of 4\,$\times$\,4\,mm.}
Open almost any small drone, game controller or phone and you will find a chip like the one in the photograph: a MEMS inertial sensor. Inside, microscopic silicon structures move. The accelerometer has a proof mass on tiny springs whose deflection is read as a change of capacitance. The gyroscope keeps a structure vibrating at kilohertz and measures the Coriolis force that appears when the chip rotates. Such sensors cost about a dollar. Their price and their small size are what made the modern quadrotor possible.

They are also noisy. Their noise is specified as a density — micro-$g$ or degrees per second per $\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ — because the noise you see depends on how much bandwidth you let through. That is \cref{fig:noise-bode} in a data sheet. On a drone the electronic noise is the smaller problem: the propellers shake the frame at their rotation frequency, a few hundred hertz, and the sensor measures that too. Flight-controller software such as Betaflight or PX4 therefore runs whole banks of filters: low-pass filters, and \emph{notch} filters that follow the motor speed and cut out exactly the frequency of the propellers. Tuning those filters is a large part of tuning a racing drone, and it is the same trade-off you just explored: every filter removes noise and adds delay.
\end{curiosity}

\begin{exercises}
\exercise[1] Compute $\alpha$ of \cref{eq:noise-filter} for $f_c = \SI{5}{Hz}$ at the controller rate of \SI{250}{Hz}, and the fluctuation of the D term from \cref{eq:noise-filtered}.
\answer{$T_f = \SI{31.8}{ms}$, $\alpha = 0.888$; $\sigma_D = 7 \cdot 2.5 \cdot 0.112 \cdot \sqrt{2/1.888} = \SI{2.0}{N}$.}
\exercise[1] The altimeter noise doubles to \SI{2}{cm}. What are the fluctuations of the D term without a filter and with the \SI{20}{Hz} filter?
\answer{Everything is linear in $\sigma$: \SI{49.5}{N} and \SI{12.8}{N}.}
\exercise[1] Use the noise budget of the engineer's view: the motors tolerate \SI{0.5}{N} of fluctuation and the loop needs a \SI{10}{Hz} filter. Which altimeter noise is allowed?
\answer{$\sigma \le 0.5/(2\pi \cdot 10 \cdot 7) = \SI{1.1}{mm}$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} Show that the phase lag of the first-order filter, divided by $\omega$, tends to $T_f$ for $\omega T_f \ll 1$: a slow sinusoid is delayed by the time constant. What is the delay, in the same sense, at the cutoff frequency $\omega = 1/T_f$?
\answer{$\arctan(\omega T_f)/\omega \to T_f$. At the cutoff the lag is $45^\circ = \pi/4$, a delay of $\pi T_f/4 = 0.79\,T_f$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} Averaging instead of filtering. Replace the filter by the mean of the last $N$ raw derivatives. Show that this equals $(y_k - y_{k-N})/(N\Delta t)$, find the fluctuation of the D term and the delay, and choose $N$ to match the noise of the \SI{20}{Hz} filter.
\answer{The sum telescopes. $\sigma_D = \sqrt2K_d\sigma/(N\Delta t)$; delay about $N\Delta t/2$. $24.7/N = 6.4$ gives $N = 4$: a delay of \SI{8}{ms}, the same as $T_f$ of the filter.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{derive} Repeat Step~3 of Example~\ref{ex:noise-rect} for motors twice as strong, $u_{max} = \SI{48.8}{N}$. Which way is the bias now, and why?
\answer{$b = 1.575$: $\Phi(b) = 0.942$, $\varphi(b) = 0.115$. $\mathbb{E} = 48.8 \cdot 0.058 + 9.81 \cdot 0.596 + 24.7 \cdot 0.254 = 2.81 + 5.85 + 6.28 = \SI{14.9}{N}$: an even larger upward bias, because now almost only the lower limit clips. The bias vanishes when the limits are symmetric about the hover thrust, $u_{max} = \SI{19.6}{N}$.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{fly} In Lesson~8, without a filter, read the altitude after \SI{6}{s}. Then set the noise to \SI{0.5}{cm} and reset. Predict the new offset first: repeat Steps 3–5 of Example~\ref{ex:noise-rect} with $s = \SI{12.4}{N}$.
\answer{With $s = 12.4$: at $\mu = 9.81$, $a = -0.79$, $b = 1.18$; the clipped mean is \SI{10.6}{N}, a bias of \SI{0.8}{N}; $\mu^* \approx 8.6$, so an offset of about \SI{12}{cm} — less than a third for half the noise.}
\exercise[2]\exkind{code} In \src{src/control/pid.ts} the filter uses \texttt{lowPassAlpha(g.dFilterHz, dt)}. Replace the first-order filter by two of them in series (a second-order filter) with the same cutoff. Using \cref{thm:noise-freq}, predict the gain at \SI{125}{Hz} for a \SI{20}{Hz} cutoff before and after, and the phase lag at \SI{0.5}{Hz}. Fly the lesson with both.
\answer{First order: $|H| = 1/\sqrt{1 + 6.25^2} = 0.158$; second order: $0.025$. Lag at \SI{0.5}{Hz}: $1.4^\circ$ and $2.9^\circ$. Six times less noise at the top of the band for twice the (small) delay.}
\exercise[3]\exkind{derive} Correlated noise. Let the altimeter error be $n_k = \rho\,n_{k-1} + w_k$ with white $w_k$ and $0 < \rho < 1$, scaled so that $\operatorname{Var} n_k = \sigma^2$. Show that $\operatorname{Var}(n_k - n_{k-1}) = 2\sigma^2(1 - \rho)$, and conclude that slowly varying errors hardly disturb the D term. What do they disturb instead?
\answer{$\operatorname{Cov}(n_k, n_{k-1}) = \rho\sigma^2$, so the variance of the difference is $2\sigma^2 - 2\rho\sigma^2$. For $\rho \to 1$ (a drifting bias) the D term sees nothing; the error passes straight into P and I, which follow it faithfully — the drone flies at the wrong altitude without noticing.}
\end{exercises}
