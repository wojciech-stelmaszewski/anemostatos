\section{The experiment}

The lesson keeps $K_d = 7$ and flies a small square wave while you raise $K_p$ through 30, 100, 200 and 400. \Cref{fig:edge-runs} shows what happens. At 30 the response is crisp. At 100 it rings a little after every step. At 200 the ringing hardly decays. At 400 the drone oscillates by itself, and the square wave is barely recognisable.

\widefig{edge_runs}{The altitude with $K_d = 7$ and increasing $K_p$. Near the edge the loop \enquote{rings} longer and longer; past it the oscillation sustains itself, bounded only by the motor limits.}{fig:edge-runs}

Nothing in the model of \lessonref{les:damper} predicts this. For $m\ddot x + K_d\dot x + K_p x = 0$ every positive $K_p$ and $K_d$ gives a stable loop; a larger $K_p$ merely lowers the damping ratio $\zeta = K_d/(2\sqrt{K_p m})$. At $K_p = 400$, $\zeta = 0.18$ — lightly damped, but stable. The simulator disagrees because the drone is not an ideal mass: its motors lag.\pitfall{When a flight contradicts a formula, the formula is not wrong: one of its assumptions is. Find out which.}

\section{The motor lag as a third pole}

The motors follow the command with a first-order lag: the thrust $T$ chases the command $u$ with the time constant $\tau_m = \SI{30}{ms}$,
\begin{equation}
  \tau_m\dot T + T = u .
\end{equation}
In \lessonref{les:spring} we treated the lag as a small delay and found that it destabilises the P-only loop. Here we keep it exactly.\innumbers{Time constants of real thrusters: a small racing motor, 20 to \SI{40}{ms}; a large propeller, \SI{100}{ms} or more; a jet engine, seconds.}

\begin{example}[The loop with its motors]
\label{ex:edge-cubic}
Derive the equation of the P–D loop with the motor lag, and the largest stable $K_p$.

\textbf{Step 1 — the pieces.} With exact feedforward and $x = y - r$: the drone, $m\ddot x = T'$, where $T' = T - mg$ is the thrust above hover; the motors, $\tau_m\dot T' + T' = u'$; the controller, $u' = -K_p x - K_d\dot x$.

\textbf{Step 2 — eliminate the thrust.} From the drone $T' = m\ddot x$ and $\dot T' = m\dddot x$. Insert both into the motor equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge-cubic}
  \tau_m m\,\dddot x + m\,\ddot x + K_d\,\dot x + K_p\,x = 0,
  \qquad
  \tau_m m\,s^3 + m\,s^2 + K_d\,s + K_p = 0 .
\end{equation}
For $\tau_m = 0$ this is the mass–spring–damper. The lag has added a third pole.

\textbf{Step 3 — Routh–Hurwitz.} All coefficients are positive; \cref{thm:spring-routh}\,(ii) further requires $a_2a_1 > a_3a_0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edge-limit}
  m\,K_d > \tau_m m\,K_p \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad K_p < \frac{K_d}{\tau_m} = \frac{7}{0.03} = 233 .
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4 — the oscillation at the edge.} At $K_p = 233$ the cubic has the roots $s = \pm j\omega$ with $\omega^2 = a_1/a_3 = K_d/(\tau_m m) = 233$ (the imaginary part of the cubic at $s = j\omega$):\tip[-78pt]{At its edge the cubic $a_3s^3 + a_2s^2 + a_1s + a_0$ has the roots $\pm j\sqrt{a_1/a_3}$. The gain and the frequency of the edge, without solving anything.} $\omega = \SI{15.3}{rad/s}$, a period of \SI{0.41}{s}.

\textbf{Step 5 — interpret.} The mass has dropped out of \cref{eq:edge-limit}: the limit is a contest between the damper and the lag alone. And the lesson of \cref{eq:spring-lag} is confirmed exactly: a lagging spring acts like a negative damper of size $K_p\tau_m$, and the loop is stable while the real damper is larger, $K_d > K_p\tau_m$.
\end{example}

\subsection{The other lags}
The motors are not the only thing that is late. The D term passes through a \SI{20}{Hz} filter ($T_f = \SI{8}{ms}$, \lessonref{les:noise}), and the controller samples and holds (\SI{2}{ms} of delay, \lessonref{les:slow}). Each adds a pole or a delay, and the polynomial grows to degree five. It can no longer be handled by hand, but a computer finds its roots in a moment.\tip{A crude estimate lumps the small lags into one: $30 + 8 + 2 = \SI{40}{ms}$ gives $K_p < 7/0.04 = 175$. Too pessimistic here, because the filter sits on the D path only — but the right size.}

\begin{example}[Predicting the ringing, gain by gain]
\label{ex:edge-ringing}

\begin{keyidea}[Every lag sets a speed limit]
The motors cannot follow faster than about $1/\tau_m \approx \SI{33}{rad/s}$. A loop that tries to be faster than its slowest lag becomes unstable. Delays (\lessonref{les:slow}), filters (\lessonref{les:noise}) and actuator lags all add up to the same limit. Gains can be chosen; the lags of the hardware cannot.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{A map of the stable gains}
The edge depends on $K_d$ too: \cref{eq:edge-limit} is a straight line in the plane of the two gains, and \cref{fig:edge-region} shows the whole region. A stronger damper allows a stiffer spring, which is why the tuning procedures of practice raise $K_p$ and $K_d$ in turns. But the line bends over: with the D filter and the hold in the model, more $K_d$ buys less and less $K_p$, and the damper itself runs into the noise of \lessonref{les:noise}. This game ends too.

\section{About Ziegler and Nichols}

In 1942 John Ziegler and Nathaniel Nichols of the Taylor Instrument Company published a recipe that is still taught everywhere:\didyouknow{Ziegler and Nichols found their numbers by experiment, on pneumatic controllers. Their aim was a \emph{quarter decay}: each overshoot a quarter of the one before. By today's taste that is far too lively.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item switch off I and D and raise $K_p$ until the loop oscillates with constant amplitude; call that gain the \emph{ultimate gain} $K_u$, and the period $T_u$;
  \item set $K_p = 0.6\,K_u$, $K_i = 1.2\,K_u/T_u$, $K_d = 0.075\,K_uT_u$.
\end{enumerate}
It asks nothing but one experiment, which explains its success.

\begin{example}[The recipe on a plant it was made for]
\label{ex:edge-zn}
A process has three equal lags, each with a time constant of \SI{1}{s}, in series (three tanks, say, or a heater, a wall and a sensor): $(\tau\frac{\dd}{\dd t} + 1)^3\,y = u$. Apply the recipe.

\textbf{Step 1 — P control.} $u = K(r - y)$ gives the characteristic polynomial $(s + 1)^3 + K = s^3 + 3s^2 + 3s + (1 + K)$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the ultimate gain.} Routh–Hurwitz: stable while $3 \cdot 3 > 1 + K$, i.e.\ $K < 8$. So $K_u = 8$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the ultimate period.} At the limit $\omega^2 = a_1/a_3 = 3$: $\omega = \SI{1.73}{rad/s}$, $T_u = 2\pi/1.73 = \SI{3.63}{s}$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the gains.} $K_p = 4.8$, $K_i = 1.2 \cdot 8/3.63 = 2.64$, $K_d = 0.075 \cdot 8 \cdot 3.63 = 2.18$.
\end{example}

Now try step 1 on a hovering drone. With P alone the cubic \eqref{eq:edge-cubic} has $K_d = 0$, so $a_1 = 0$ and $a_2a_1 = 0 < a_3a_0$ for \emph{every} positive $K_p$: the loop is unstable at every gain (Example~\ref{ex:spring-cubic}). There is no gain below which it is calm, so there is no $K_u$ to find. The three-tank process has a stable range $0 < K < 8$ because it is stable on its own; the drone, left alone, falls.

\begin{watchout}[Heuristics come with assumptions]
The Ziegler–Nichols rules were derived for plants that are stable on their own and have some delay: temperatures, levels, pressures in process plants — the systems Ziegler and Nichols worked with. A hovering drone is a \emph{double integrator}: without control it simply falls. Always ask what a recipe assumes about the plant before you use it.
\end{watchout}

\section{The engineer's view: gain margin}

\subsection{How far from the edge}
\Lessonref{les:slow} measured the distance to instability in \emph{delay}: the phase margin. This lesson measures it in \emph{gain}.\recall{Decibels are $20\log_{10}$ of a ratio. A factor of 2 is \SI{6}{dB}, a factor of 10 is \SI{20}{dB}, a factor of 16 is \SI{24}{dB}.}

\begin{definition}{Gain margin}
The \term{gain margin} of a stable loop is the factor by which its loop gain can be multiplied before the loop becomes unstable. In the frequency domain: if the phase of $L(j\omega)$ reaches $-180^\circ$ at the frequency $\omega_{180}$, the gain margin is $1/|L(j\omega_{180})|$.
\end{definition}

The two definitions agree. Multiplying the loop gain by $k$ leaves the phase alone; the loop oscillates by itself when $k\,L(j\omega_0) = -1$, which needs the phase $-180^\circ$ and $k = 1/|L|$ there.

For the default altitude loop (all lags included), the phase reaches $-180^\circ$ at $\omega_{180} = \SI{55}{rad/s}$, where $|L| = 0.062$: a gain margin of 16. The whole controller could be made sixteen times stronger — or the drone sixteen times lighter — before the loop shakes. That is a comfortable margin; flight control specifications usually demand at least a factor of two (\SI{6}{dB}).

\subsection{Why the margin is finite}
At high frequency the P–D controller behaves like $K_ds$ (phase $+90^\circ$), the drone like $1/(ms^2)$ ($-180^\circ$): together $-90^\circ$, safely away from $-180^\circ$. The ideal loop therefore has an \emph{infinite} gain margin, which is the statement that the mass–spring–damper is stable for all gains. Each lag contributes up to another $-90^\circ$ at high frequency, and a delay contributes without limit.\recall{Phase at high frequency: every pole gives $-90^\circ$, every zero in the left half-plane $+90^\circ$, a delay $-\omega T$ without end.} With the motor lag the phase approaches $-180^\circ$; with the filter or the hold as well it crosses, and the margin becomes finite. The count is simple:
\begin{keyidea}[Count the excess of poles]
A loop whose transfer function has two more poles than zeros can be stable at any gain. Three or more, or any delay, and a high enough gain destabilises it. Real actuators and real computers always supply the third.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Too little gain}
The default loop has a second, less expected limit. It contains an integral, and \cref{thm:integral-stability} requires $K_i < K_pK_d/m$. Scale the whole controller by $k$: the condition becomes $kK_i < k^2K_pK_d/m$, i.e.\ $k > mK_i/(K_pK_d) = 0.8/70 = 0.011$. Below one ninetieth of its nominal gain the loop is unstable again. A loop that is stable only between two gains is called \term{conditionally stable}.\didyouknow{Conditionally stable loops frighten operators: turning the gain \emph{down} to calm a shaking loop can make it worse.} It matters when an actuator saturates (saturation acts like a reduced gain) or loses power — one more reason for the anti-windup of \lessonref{les:windup}.

\begin{example}[An autopilot and its servo]
\label{ex:edge-aircraft}
A small aircraft holds its pitch angle $\theta$ with the elevator. Around the operating point the elevator deflection $\delta$ produces a pitch acceleration $\ddot\theta = M_\delta\,\delta$ with $M_\delta = \SI{10}{s^{-2}}$. The elevator servo follows its command with a lag of $\tau = \SI{50}{ms}$. The autopilot uses $\delta_{cmd} = -K_p\theta - K_d\dot\theta$ with $K_d = \SI{1}{s}$. How large may $K_p$ be?

\textbf{Step 1 — the same three equations.} Body: $\ddot\theta = M_\delta\delta$. Servo: $\tau\dot\delta + \delta = \delta_{cmd}$. Controller as given. They are the drone's, with $1/M_\delta$ in place of $m$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the cubic.} $\tau\,\dddot\theta + \ddot\theta + M_\delta K_d\,\dot\theta + M_\delta K_p\,\theta = 0$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the limit.} $M_\delta K_d > \tau M_\delta K_p$: $K_p < K_d/\tau = 1/0.05 = 20$ (radians of elevator per radian of pitch).

\textbf{Step 4 — the frequency at the edge.} $\omega^2 = M_\delta K_d/\tau = 200$: \SI{14}{rad/s}, about \SI{2.3}{Hz}.

\textbf{Step 5 — the practical meaning.} Wings and fuselage bend at a few hertz.\innumbers{First bending frequencies: the wing of an airliner, 1 to \SI{3}{Hz}; of a fighter, 5 to 10; a large rocket, around 1.} An autopilot gain set close to this limit would shake the aircraft near its own structural frequencies. Designers keep a gain margin of at least two — here $K_p \le 10$ — and then check the structure separately (Lesson~IV.8 does it for a rocket).
\end{example}

\subsection{In formal terms}

\begin{theorem}[The edge of the altitude loop]
\label{thm:edge-limit}
For $m, \tau_m, K_d > 0$ the loop \eqref{eq:edge-cubic} is asymptotically stable if and only if $0 < K_p < K_d/\tau_m$. At $K_p = K_d/\tau_m$ it has the poles $\pm j\sqrt{K_d/(\tau_m m)}$ and $-1/\tau_m$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\Cref{thm:spring-routh}\,(ii) on $\tau_m m s^3 + m s^2 + K_d s + K_p$ gives the condition. At the boundary the polynomial factors as $(\tau_m s + 1)(m s^2 + K_d/\tau_m)$, as multiplication shows.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[High gain and the excess of poles]
\label{thm:edge-asymptotes}
Let $L(s) = k\,N(s)/D(s)$ with polynomials $N$ of degree $n_z$ and $D$ of degree $n_p > n_z$, all zeros of $N$ in the open left half-plane. As $k \to \infty$, $n_z$ closed-loop poles tend to the zeros of $N$, and the other $n_p - n_z$ tend to infinity along straight lines at the angles $(2\ell + 1)\,180^\circ/(n_p - n_z)$, $\ell = 0, 1, \dots$. Hence for $n_p - n_z \ge 3$ the loop is unstable for all sufficiently large $k$, while for $n_p - n_z \le 2$ it need not be. (Evans, 1948; Franklin, Powell and Emami-Naeini, 2019, ch.~5.)
\end{theorem}

For the ideal P–D loop $n_p - n_z = 2 - 1 = 1$: one pole goes to the zero at $-K_p/K_d$, the other to $-\infty$ along the real axis, the overdamped picture of \lessonref{les:damper}. With the motor lag the excess is 2 and the asymptotes are vertical, at $\pm90^\circ$: stable for all gains \emph{if the ratio $K_p/K_d$ is kept} — \cref{thm:edge-limit} concerns raising $K_p$ alone, which moves the zero. With the D filter as well the excess is 3, and the gain margin is finite whatever the ratio; and with a delay no finite count applies: every loop with a delay has a finite gain margin.

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The ideal mass–spring–damper is stable for any positive gains; real loops are not.
  \item A first-order actuator lag $\tau_m$ makes the loop third order, stable only for $K_p < K_d/\tau_m$; at the edge it oscillates at $\sqrt{K_d/(\tau_mm)}$.
  \item Filters, delays and sampling lower the edge further: from 233 to 201 for the default $K_d$.
  \item Gain margin: the factor by which the loop gain can grow before instability. It is finite whenever the loop has three more poles than zeros, or a delay.
  \item Tuning recipes such as Ziegler–Nichols assume a type of plant; a hovering drone is not that type.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{An aircraft that could not fly by itself}
\photo{x29}{The Grumman X-29 over the Mojave Desert. Its forward-swept wings made it so unstable that no pilot could have flown it unaided.}
The X-29, flown by NASA and the US Air Force from 1984 to 1992, looks as if it had been assembled backwards: its wings sweep forward and a pair of small canard wings sits in front of them. The design reduced drag and improved agility at high angles of attack, but it had a price. The aircraft was aerodynamically unstable in pitch — a small nose-up disturbance made the nose rise further, faster and faster, like a pole in the right half-plane.

A pilot cannot correct such an instability: the time to double the disturbance was far shorter than human reaction time. So the X-29 flew with three redundant digital flight-control computers that corrected its attitude about 40 times per second. The whole aircraft relied on a loop running well inside its stability margins; a computer failure would have meant losing the aircraft within a fraction of a second.

What the X-29 made extreme is now routine. Modern fighters are designed slightly unstable for agility and rely on their flight-control computers, and the lesson's rule applies to all of them: the control loop must be fast enough to catch the instability — and its actuators and computers fast enough for the loop.
\end{curiosity}

\answer{With gains $k(10, 0.8, 7)$, $m = 1$, $\tau_m = 0.03$: $7k > 0.3k$ always; $10k(7k - 0.3k) > 0.8k$ gives $k > 0.8/67 = 0.012$. No upper limit in this model (the excess of poles is two and the ratio of the gains is kept); the upper limit of 16 comes from the D filter and the hold.}
