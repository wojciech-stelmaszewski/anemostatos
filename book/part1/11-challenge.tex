\section{The wind of Anemostatos}

The simulated air velocity $\vec w(t)$ is the sum of three parts:\innumbers{The turbulence of this lesson, $\sigma_w = \SI{1.2}{m/s}$, is a light breeze. Its gusts, up to \SI{10}{m/s}, are about the limit that a small camera drone is rated for.}
\begin{equation}
  \vec w(t) = \vec w_{mean} + \vec w_{turb}(t) + \vec w_{gust}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Turbulence: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process}
Turbulence is random but not arbitrary: the wind now is similar to the wind a moment ago. White noise (\lessonref{les:noise}) would be a poor model — it forgets everything between one instant and the next. The simulator models each component of the turbulence as a velocity that is constantly pulled back towards zero and constantly kicked by white noise:\didyouknow{The same equation models interest rates in finance and the voltage of a nerve cell between two spikes: anything that is pulled towards a mean and jostled at random.}

\begin{definition}{Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:challenge-ou}
  \dd w = -\frac{w}{\tau_w}\,\dd t + \sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{2}{\tau_w}}\,\dd W ,
\end{equation}
where $\dd W$ is the increment of white noise over $\dd t$ (a Gaussian number of variance $\dd t$). The first term is a relaxation with the \term{correlation time} $\tau_w$; the second a random drive, scaled so that in the long run the standard deviation of $w$ is $\sigma_w$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[One step of turbulence]
\label{ex:challenge-ou}
Derive the rule that advances \cref{eq:challenge-ou} by one simulation step, and check that it keeps the right size. The lesson uses $\sigma_w = \SI{1.2}{m/s}$, $\tau_w = \SI{1.5}{s}$, $\Delta t = \SI{1}{ms}$.

\textbf{Step 1 — the deterministic part.} Without the noise, $\dot w = -w/\tau_w$ decays exponentially: after $\Delta t$ the value is $w\,e^{-\Delta t/\tau_w}$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the random part.} The kicks received during the step add a Gaussian number; call its standard deviation $q$. So $w_{k+1} = a\,w_k + q\,\xi_k$ with $a = e^{-\Delta t/\tau_w}$ and $\xi_k$ a standard Gaussian.

\textbf{Step 3 — choose $q$ to keep the variance.} $w_k$ and $\xi_k$ are independent, so\recall{For independent $X$ and $Y$: $\operatorname{Var}(aX + qY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}X + q^2\operatorname{Var}Y$.} $\operatorname{Var}w_{k+1} = a^2\operatorname{Var}w_k + q^2$. If the variance is to stay at $\sigma_w^2$: $\sigma_w^2 = a^2\sigma_w^2 + q^2$, hence $q = \sigma_w\sqrt{1 - a^2}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:challenge-ou-step}
  w_{k+1} = w_k\,e^{-\Delta t/\tau_w} + \sigma_w\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\Delta t/\tau_w}}\;\xi_k .
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4 — numbers.} $a = e^{-0.001/1.5} = 0.99933$ and $q = 1.2\sqrt{1 - 0.99867} = \SI{0.044}{m/s}$. Each millisecond the wind keeps 99.93\,\% of its value and receives a kick of about \SI{4}{cm/s}.

\textbf{Step 5 — memory.} After a time $t$ the wind still \enquote{remembers} the fraction $e^{-t/\tau_w}$ of its value: 51\,\% after one second, 3.6\,\% after five. Values more than a few $\tau_w$ apart are practically independent.
\end{example}

The recursion \eqref{eq:challenge-ou-step} is exact for any step size, and it is how \src{src/sim/wind.ts} advances the turbulence.\aside{The OU process is the continuous-time version of the AR(1) model of statistics, and the simplest model of a velocity subject to friction and random forces — Brownian motion of a heavy particle.}

\subsection{Gusts: the one-minus-cosine}
On top of the turbulence come discrete gusts. They arrive at random times (a Poisson process, 14 per minute here),\recall{A Poisson process: events arrive independently at a constant average rate. Fourteen per minute means one every \SI{4.3}{s} on average — with long gaps and clusters.} each with a random direction, peak speed $A$ (4 to \SI{10}{m/s}) and duration $T_g$ (0.6 to \SI{2.5}{s}), and each follows the classic shape of aviation engineering:
\begin{equation}
  w_{gust}(t) = \frac{A}{2}\left(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi(t - t_0)}{T_g}\right),\qquad t_0 \le t \le t_0 + T_g .
\end{equation}
It starts and ends smoothly, which is what a real gust does and what makes it a fair test: no infinite accelerations, only a shove.

\subsection{From wind to force}
The drone feels the wind through drag on its velocity \emph{relative to the air}: vertically
\begin{equation}\label{eq:challenge-drag}
  d = -c_v\,|v - w|\,(v - w), \qquad c_v = \SI{0.25}{N.s^2/m^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[What a gust asks of the motors]
\label{ex:challenge-force}
A hovering drone is hit by a vertical gust of \SI{8}{m/s}. What would it take to stay put?

\textbf{Step 1 — the force.} With $v = 0$: $|d| = 0.25 \cdot 8^2 = \SI{16}{N}$ — more than one and a half times the weight.\pitfall{Drag grows with the square of the wind. A gust twice as fast pushes four times as hard.}

\textbf{Step 2 — a downward gust.} To hold position the motors must supply $9.81 + 16 = \SI{25.8}{N}$. They have \SI{24.4}{N}. The drone will sink, whatever the controller does.

\textbf{Step 3 — an upward gust.} The motors would have to supply $9.81 - 16 = \SI{-6.2}{N}$: a downward pull. Propellers cannot pull. Even with the motors off, the drone is lifted with $16 - 9.81 = \SI{6.2}{N}$.

\textbf{Step 4 — what saves it.} As the drone starts to move with the air, the relative velocity and with it the force fall quickly (quadratically), and the gust is short. The worst gust of the challenge, \SI{8.7}{m/s} downwards, pushes the default-tuned drone \SI{98}{cm} off its setpoint.

\textbf{Step 5 — the lesson.} For the strongest gusts the limit is the vehicle, not the tuning. No controller can do better than saturate in the right direction at once. For the moderate gusts and the turbulence, the tuning decides.
\end{example}

\section{How a loop rejects a disturbance}

Where does the error come from, and what can the gains do about it? Treat the gust as a force $d(t)$ on the mass. The tools are those of Lessons~I.8 and~I.9: ask what the loop does to a \emph{sinusoidal} force of each frequency.

Write the closed loop with the full PID ($r$ constant, exact feedforward, and for a moment without the motor lag): $m\ddot y = -K_p y - K_i\!\int\! y\,\dd t - K_d\dot y + d$, measuring $y$ from the setpoint. Differentiating once,
\begin{equation}
  m\dddot y + K_d\ddot y + K_p\dot y + K_i y = \dot d .
\end{equation}
By \cref{thm:noise-freq}, a force $d = \sin\omega t$ produces a motion of the amplitude $|H_d(j\omega)|$, with the transfer function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:challenge-sens}
  H_d(s) = \frac{s}{m s^3 + K_d s^2 + K_p s + K_i} = \frac{1}{m s^2 + K_d s + K_p + K_i/s} .
\end{equation}
The second form reads well: the denominator is what resists the force — inertia ($ms^2$), damper, spring, and integral. At each frequency one of them dominates.\tip{Read a transfer function by asking which term of its denominator is the largest at the frequency you care about. The others hardly matter there.}

\begin{example}[The response to a slow and to a fast force]
\label{ex:challenge-freq}
A force of \SI{1}{N} amplitude oscillates at \SI{0.4}{Hz}, the rhythm of the longest gusts. By how much does the drone move, with the default tune and with $K_p = 40$, $K_i = 8$, $K_d = 12$?

\textbf{Step 1 — the frequency.} $\omega = 2\pi \cdot 0.4 = \SI{2.51}{rad/s}$, $s = j\omega$, $s^2 = -6.32$.

\textbf{Step 2 — the denominator, default tune.} $ms^2 = -6.32$; $K_p = 10$; $K_ds = 17.59j$; $K_i/s = -0.32j$.\recall{$1/j = -j$. So $K_i/(j\omega) = -jK_i/\omega$: the integral's term points opposite to the damper's.} Sum: $3.68 + 17.27j$, with the magnitude $\sqrt{3.68^2 + 17.27^2} = 17.66$.

\textbf{Step 3 — the motion.} $|H_d| = 1/17.66 = \SI{0.057}{m/N}$: \SI{5.7}{cm} per newton. At this frequency the damper does most of the resisting.

\textbf{Step 4 — the stiffer tune.} $-6.32 + 40 + 30.16j - 3.18j = 33.68 + 26.98j$, magnitude 43.2: \SI{2.3}{cm} per newton, two and a half times less.

\textbf{Step 5 — a fast force.} At \SI{3}{Hz}, $\omega = 18.8$ and $ms^2 = -355$: the inertia term dwarfs everything a reasonable gain can add. Both tunes move by about $1/355 = \SI{0.3}{cm}$ per newton — what the free mass would do. The controller is irrelevant there.

\textbf{Step 6 — a constant force.} For $\omega \to 0$ the term $K_i/s$ grows without bound and $|H_d| \to 0$: the integral removes a constant load completely (\lessonref{les:integral}). Without it $|H_d| \to 1/K_p$, the droop of \lessonref{les:droop}.
\end{example}

\simfig{challenge_sens}{How much a disturbing force of each frequency moves the drone: $|H_d(j\omega)|$ of \cref{eq:challenge-sens}, with the motor lag and the D filter included. The tuned loop is stiffer in the band of the gusts (grey); at high frequency no tune can beat the inertia of the drone (dashed: the free mass).}{fig:challenge-sens}

\Cref{fig:challenge-sens} draws the whole curve. Three regions are visible.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Slow disturbances} (left) are crushed by the integral.
  \item \textbf{Middle frequencies}, where the gusts live, are resisted by the stiffness and damping of the loop. This is the region that tuning changes.
  \item \textbf{Fast disturbances} (right) are too quick for any controller: there the response is that of the free mass, $1/(m\omega^2)$. Only inertia helps.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keyidea}[Disturbance rejection is bandwidth]
A feedback loop can only reject disturbances that are slower than itself. To fight faster gusts you need a faster loop: higher $K_p$ with enough $K_d$ to damp it and enough $K_i$ to remove what remains. Every earlier lesson sets a limit on how far you can go: saturation (\lessonref{les:windup}), sensor noise amplified by D (\lessonref{les:noise}), delay (\lessonref{les:slow}) and actuator lag (\lessonref{les:edge}).
\end{keyidea}

\section{A tune that wins — and a prediction of the score}

\begin{example}[Designing the orange run]
\label{ex:challenge-tune}
Choose gains that at least halve the error of the default tune, and check them against every limit of Part~I.

\textbf{Step 1 — stiffness.} \Cref{fig:challenge-sens}: in the gust band the response is roughly $1/\sqrt{K_p^2 + (K_d\omega)^2}$. Take $K_p = 40$, four times the default.

\textbf{Step 2 — damping} (\lessonref{les:damper}). Critical damping for $K_p = 40$ is $K_d = 2\sqrt{40} = 12.6$. Take $K_d = 12$: $\zeta = 0.95$.

\textbf{Step 3 — integral} (\lessonref{les:integral}). The integral time $K_p/K_i$ should stay well above the loop's own time scale $1/\omega_n = \SI{0.16}{s}$ and far below the Routh limit $K_pK_d/m = 480$. $K_i = 8$ gives $K_p/K_i = \SI{5}{s}$.

\textbf{Step 4 — the edge} (\lessonref{les:edge}). $K_d/\tau_m = 12/0.03 = 400$: $K_p = 40$ is a factor of ten inside.

\textbf{Step 5 — delay} (\lessonref{les:slow}). The crossover moves up to about \SI{12}{rad/s}; the motor lag then costs $\arctan(0.03 \cdot 12) = 20^\circ$. With the D filter opened to \SI{30}{Hz} the phase margin is $50^\circ$, and the delay margin $0.87/11.9 = \SI{73}{ms}$ — half of the default's.

\textbf{Step 6 — noise} (\lessonref{les:noise}). No sensor noise in this lesson. With \SI{1}{cm} of it, \cref{eq:noise-simple} gives $2\pi \cdot 30 \cdot 12 \cdot 0.01 = \SI{23}{N}$ in the D term — far too much: on a real drone this tune would need a better altimeter.

\textbf{Step 7 — fly.} RMS error \SI{9.9}{cm} against \SI{21.0}{cm}. The thrust changes about 50\,\% more from sample to sample, and the motors are at a limit 4.6\,\% of the time instead of 2.0\,\%.\tip[-20pt]{Nothing is free. The winning tune works the motors harder and would not survive a noisy sensor. Always report what a better score cost.}
\end{example}

\section{The engineer's view: sensitivity}

\subsection{One function for all disturbances}
Compare the drone with and without feedback. A force $d$ moves the free mass according to $P(s) = 1/(ms^2)$. With the controller $C(s) = K_p + K_i/s + K_ds$ in the loop, \cref{eq:challenge-sens} can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:challenge-S}
  H_d(s) = \frac{1}{ms^2 + C(s)} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{ms^2}}_{P(s)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{1 + L(s)}}_{S(s)}, \qquad L(s) = \frac{C(s)}{ms^2} .
\end{equation}
$L$ is the loop transfer function of \lessonref{les:slow}. The factor $S = 1/(1 + L)$ is called the \term{sensitivity function}: at each frequency it says by how much feedback has reduced the effect of a disturbance, compared with no feedback at all.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Where the loop gain is large, $|L| \gg 1$: $|S| \approx 1/|L|$. The disturbance is divided by the loop gain. This is Black's amplifier of Chapter~0 again.
  \item Where the loop gain is small, $|L| \ll 1$: $|S| \approx 1$. Feedback does nothing.\innumbers{The default tune at \SI{0.4}{Hz}: the free mass would move $1/(m\omega^2) = \SI{16}{cm}$ per newton, the loop moves 5.7. Feedback has cut the disturbance to about a third.}
  \item Near the crossover, $|L| \approx 1$, the two add as complex numbers, and if the phase margin is small $1 + L$ is nearly zero: $|S| > 1$. There feedback makes disturbances \emph{worse}. In \cref{fig:challenge-sens} the tuned curve rises slightly above the free mass near \SI{2}{Hz}.
\end{itemize}
The crossover frequency is therefore the \term{bandwidth} of disturbance rejection, and the last point is the beginning of a deep fact: the improvement at low frequency must be paid for with a deterioration somewhere else (\cref{thm:challenge-bode}).

\subsection{From a spectrum to an RMS}
The score of the challenge is an RMS value under random wind.\recall{RMS: the root of the mean of the squares. For a signal with zero mean it is the standard deviation.} For a linear loop and a random disturbance with a known \emph{power spectral density} $\Phi_d(\omega)$ — its power per unit of frequency — the variance of the output is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:challenge-rms}
  \sigma_y^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |H_d(j\omega)|^2\,\Phi_d(\omega)\,\dd\omega ,
\end{equation}
the continuous counterpart of \cref{thm:noise-variance}. For the turbulence \eqref{eq:challenge-ou}, $\Phi_w(\omega) = 2\sigma_w^2\tau_w/(1 + \omega^2\tau_w^2)$: flat up to $1/\tau_w$, then falling. The integral says what \cref{fig:challenge-sens} shows: the error comes from the band where the disturbance still has power \emph{and} the loop no longer rejects it. Aircraft designers compute gust loads this way, from standard turbulence spectra.

\begin{example}[An airliner meets a gust]
\label{ex:challenge-aircraft}
An airliner cruises at $V = \SI{230}{m/s}$ where the air density is $\rho = \SI{0.38}{kg/m^3}$. Its wing loading is $W/S = \SI{6000}{N/m^2}$, and its lift grows with the angle of attack at the rate $a = 5$ per radian. It flies into an upward gust of $U = \SI{10}{m/s}$. How hard are the passengers pushed into their seats?

\textbf{Step 1 — the mechanism.} A wing feels the wind through its angle of attack, not through drag. An upward gust tilts the oncoming air by $\Delta\alpha \approx U/V = 10/230 = \SI{0.043}{rad}$ ($2.5^\circ$).

\textbf{Step 2 — the extra lift.} Lift per unit of wing area is $\tfrac12\rho V^2 a\,\alpha$, so the increment is $\tfrac12\rho V^2 a\,U/V = \tfrac12\rho V a U$.

\textbf{Step 3 — in units of weight.} The \emph{load factor} increment is that lift divided by the wing loading:
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta n = \frac{\rho\,V\,a\,U}{2\,(W/S)} = \frac{0.38 \cdot 230 \cdot 5 \cdot 10}{2 \cdot 6000} = 0.36 .
\end{equation*}

\textbf{Step 4 — read it.} The gust adds \SI{0.36}{g}.\innumbers{Airliners are certified for load factors from $-1$ to $+2.5$. A bump of \SI{0.36}{g} is firm turbulence: the coffee stays in the cup, barely.} The force is \emph{linear} in the gust speed and in the flight speed, where the drone's was quadratic in the gust alone — a different plant, the same question. No autopilot loop can remove it: the gust is over in a fraction of a second. Airliners that alleviate gusts do it by feedforward, deflecting spoilers and ailerons when accelerometers near the nose feel the gust arrive.
\end{example}

\subsection{In formal terms}

\begin{theorem}[The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process]
\label{thm:challenge-ou}
Let $w_0$ be Gaussian with mean zero and variance $\sigma_w^2$, and let $w_{k+1}$ follow \cref{eq:challenge-ou-step} with independent standard Gaussian $\xi_k$. Then every $w_k$ is Gaussian with mean zero and variance $\sigma_w^2$, and
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb{E}[w_k\,w_{k+n}] = \sigma_w^2\,e^{-n\Delta t/\tau_w} .
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian. The variance is preserved by Step~3 of Example~\ref{ex:challenge-ou}. For the correlation, $w_{k+n} = a^n w_k$ plus kicks that are independent of $w_k$, so $\mathbb{E}[w_kw_{k+n}] = a^n\sigma_w^2$.
\end{proof}

The theorem does not depend on the step size: a simulation at \SI{1}{kHz} and one at \SI{100}{Hz} produce turbulence with the same statistics. A naive Euler step of \cref{eq:challenge-ou} would not have this property.\pitfall{If the statistics of a simulated wind depend on the step size, changing the simulation rate changes the wind — and with it every score.}

\begin{theorem}[Disturbance response of the closed loop]
\label{thm:challenge-dist}
Let the loop \eqref{eq:challenge-S} be asymptotically stable. For a force $d = d_0\sin\omega t$ the altitude tends to a sinusoid of the same frequency with the amplitude $d_0\,|P(j\omega)|\,|S(j\omega)|$. In particular the amplitude tends to zero for $\omega \to 0$ if $K_i > 0$, to $d_0/K_p$ if $K_i = 0$, and is asymptotically $d_0/(m\omega^2)$ for $\omega \to \infty$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\Cref{thm:noise-freq} applied to \cref{eq:challenge-sens}; the limits by inspection of its second form.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Bode's sensitivity integral]
\label{thm:challenge-bode}
Let $L(s)$ be a loop transfer function with at least two more poles than zeros and no poles in the open right half-plane, and let the closed loop be stable. Then
\begin{equation*}
  \int_0^\infty \ln|S(j\omega)|\,\dd\omega = 0 .
\end{equation*}
(Bode, 1945; Åström and Murray, 2021, ch.~12.)
\end{theorem}

The logarithm of $|S|$ is negative where feedback helps and positive where it hurts, and the two areas are equal. Pushing the sensitivity down in one band makes it bulge up in another, like a waterbed.\didyouknow{In a famous lecture of 1989 Gunter Stein drew this theorem as a man with a shovel: the dirt dug out of one place has to be piled up somewhere else.} For the ideal P–D loop the excess of poles is only one and the theorem does not apply; the motor lag makes it apply to every real loop. The tuning of this lesson did not escape it: it moved the bulge to higher frequencies, where the wind has little power.

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The simulator's wind is mean + Ornstein–Uhlenbeck turbulence + 1−cos gusts, all from one seed: identical wind for fair comparisons.
  \item Wind acts through drag on the relative velocity, $c_v|v - w|(v - w)$; strong gusts produce forces larger than the weight, which no tuning can hold.
  \item The loop's response to a force is $H_d = 1/(ms^2 + K_ds + K_p + K_i/s)$: the integral crushes slow disturbances, stiffness and damping resist medium ones, and nothing but inertia resists fast ones.
  \item $H_d = P \cdot S$ with the sensitivity $S = 1/(1 + L)$: feedback divides a disturbance by the loop gain where the gain is large.
  \item A faster loop scores better until saturation, noise, delay and lag stop it. Check a tune against all four.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{Machines that live in the wind}
\photo{nwtc}{A blade being fitted at the US National Wind Technology Center in Colorado. Each blade of a modern turbine is turned by its own pitch controller.}
A large wind turbine is a feedback system fighting gusts all day long. Above its rated wind speed the generator is already delivering full power, and every extra gust would overspeed the rotor and overload the drive train. So a pitch controller — at its core a PI loop on the rotor speed, with gains scheduled on the wind speed — turns the blades about their long axes to spill the excess wind. On modern machines each blade has its own actuator, and the controllers also damp the tower's swaying and reduce the fatigue loads that a lifetime of gusts would cause.

The one-minus-cosine gust of this lesson comes from aviation. Aircraft certification rules (for example EASA CS-25 and the American FAR Part 25) require airliners to be designed for \enquote{discrete gusts} of exactly this shape, with gradient lengths from about 9 to \SI{107}{m} and speeds depending on altitude. Engineers fly the aircraft model through a family of such gusts and check the loads on the wings. Many modern airliners go further: their flight-control computers deflect ailerons and spoilers as a gust arrives to relieve the wing — gust load alleviation, disturbance rejection at the scale of a jet.
\end{curiosity}
