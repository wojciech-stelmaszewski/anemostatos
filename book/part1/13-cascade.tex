\section{Divide and conquer}

\Lessonref{les:tilt} ended with the chain from motor thrusts to position: torque, angular acceleration, rate, attitude, acceleration, velocity, position. A cascade cuts that chain at the physically meaningful points and closes a loop around each piece. The outer loop's output does not go to the motors; it becomes the \emph{setpoint} of the loop inside it (\cref{fig:cascade-diagram}).

\subsection{Position: P, \SI{50}{Hz}}
\begin{equation}
  \vec v_{sp} = K_{p,pos}\,(\vec p_{sp} - \vec p),\qquad |\vec v_{sp}| \le v_{max}.
\end{equation}
A pure P is enough: it turns a position error into a desired velocity (\SI{1.2}{(m/s)/m} horizontally), limited to \SI{4}{m/s}. Position is the integral of velocity, so the next loop provides all the dynamics this one needs.

\subsection{Velocity: PID, \SI{100}{Hz}}
\begin{equation}
  \vec a_{sp} = \mathrm{PID}_{vel}(\vec v_{sp} - \vec v).
\end{equation}
It outputs a desired acceleration. Its integral is the one that holds a steady wind: a constant drag needs a constant extra acceleration command, and only an integral can supply it without a velocity error. Switch it off and the drone drifts downwind by a metre (\cref{fig:cascade-noi}).

\subsection{Thrust vector: geometry, no gains}
This block is where \enquote{to move, tilt} becomes explicit. The desired force, including the gravity feedforward, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cascade-fdes}
  \vec F_{des} = \hat m\,(\vec a_{sp} - \vec g),
\end{equation}
and since a quadrotor can only push along its body axis, the desired body axis is its direction, $\hat b_{sp} = \vec F_{des}/|\vec F_{des}|$, limited to a tilt of $35^\circ$. Together with the desired heading (yaw) this defines a full attitude setpoint $q_{sp}$. The collective thrust is the projection of $\vec F_{des}$ on the \emph{current} body axis, $T = \vec F_{des}\cdot\hat b$, so that the drone does not shoot upward while it is still tilting.

\subsection{Attitude: P, \SI{250}{Hz}}
The attitude error is the rotation that takes the current attitude to the desired one, computed with quaternions: $q_e = q^{-1}\otimes q_{sp}$. Its vector part, doubled, is (for small errors) the rotation angle times its axis, and a P law turns it into a desired body rate:
\begin{equation}
  \vec\omega_{sp} = K_{att}\cdot 2\,\sign(q_{e,w})\,\vec q_{e,xyz}.
\end{equation}
Why quaternions, not angles, is a story for \lessonref{les:geometric}.

\subsection{Rate: PID, \SI{1}{kHz}}
\begin{equation}
  \vec\alpha_{sp} = \mathrm{PID}_{rate}(\vec\omega_{sp} - \vec\omega),\qquad \vec\tau = J\,\vec\alpha_{sp}.
\end{equation}
The innermost loop controls how fast the drone rotates, from the gyroscope. Multiplying by the inertia matrix $J$ makes the gains physically meaningful (angular accelerations rather than torques). This is the loop where noise and filtering matter most.

\subsection{Mixer: linear algebra}
Finally, the four motor thrusts must produce the requested collective thrust and three torques. For the X-shaped frame with arm projection $d$ and rotor torque coefficient $c_\tau$ the relation is linear,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cascade-mixer}
  \begin{pmatrix}T\\ \tau_x\\ \tau_y\\ \tau_z\end{pmatrix} =
  \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\
    d & -d & -d & d\\
    c_\tau & -c_\tau & c_\tau & -c_\tau\\
    d & d & -d & -d
  \end{pmatrix}}_{A}
  \begin{pmatrix}f_1\\ f_2\\ f_3\\ f_4\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \vec f = A^{-1}\,(T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z)^\T ,
\end{equation}
and the \term{mixer} (control allocation) inverts it. When a motor would have to exceed its limits, the mixer first shifts all four together (trading collective thrust for attitude authority), then gives up yaw, then roll and pitch — attitude matters more than altitude for a few milliseconds.\aside{$\tau_y$ is yaw here because Anemostatos has $y$ up. Yaw torque comes from the rotors' drag: clockwise and counter-clockwise rotors twist the frame in opposite directions, and yaw is steered by speeding up one pair and slowing the other.}

\section{Watching the cascade work}

\simfig{cascade_loops}{A \SI{1}{m} step in $x$ at $t = \SI{10}{s}$, seen by the four loops (dashed: setpoint, blue: measured). Top to bottom the time scale shrinks: the position takes seconds, the velocity about a second, the pitch a few hundred milliseconds, the pitch rate tens of milliseconds. Each inner loop follows its setpoint so closely that the loop outside it barely notices it is there.}{fig:cascade-loops}

\Cref{fig:cascade-loops} is the cascade in one picture. The position loop asks for a velocity of \SI{1.2}{m/s} the instant the setpoint jumps. The velocity loop answers with an acceleration, which the thrust-vector block turns into a pitch of about $-13^\circ$ (nose down to go forward). The attitude loop asks for a pitch rate of $\SI{-110}{\degree/s}$, and the rate loop, the fastest, delivers it within a few tens of milliseconds. Then everything unwinds in reverse to brake.

\simfig{cascade_noi}{In a \SI{5}{m/s} wind the velocity loop's integral holds the drone on its setpoint (green). Without it (red) the position P alone must hold the drag, and the drone settles a metre downwind.}{fig:cascade-noi}

\begin{keyidea}[Time-scale separation]
A cascade works because each inner loop is much faster than the one around it. The outer loop can then treat the inner one as an ideal actuator: \enquote{I ask for a velocity, I get it}. Designers tune from the inside out — rate first, position last — and keep a factor of about 5–10 in bandwidth between neighbours. What happens when that rule is broken is the subject of the next lesson.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A cascade cuts the chain of integrators into pieces: position → velocity → thrust vector → attitude → rate → mixer.
  \item Each loop's output is the next loop's setpoint; the thrust-vector block turns an acceleration into an attitude.
  \item The velocity loop's integral holds steady forces such as wind.
  \item Inner loops must be much faster than outer ones; tune from the inside out.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\begin{curiosity}{Cascades everywhere}
\sidephoto{distillation}{50mm}{Distillation columns. Their temperatures, pressures and flows are held by hundreds of loops, many of them in cascade.}
The cascade was not invented for drones. In a chemical reactor the important variable is the temperature of the reaction, but the only thing an operator can move is a valve on the cooling water. Between the valve and the reaction temperature lie the flow, the temperature of the cooling jacket, and the thermal mass of the reactor — a slow chain with disturbances at every stage (the supply pressure of the water changes, the coolant warms up in summer). The standard solution since the 1940s is a cascade: an outer loop on the reactor temperature sets the setpoint of an inner loop on the jacket temperature, which sets the setpoint of a fast loop on the coolant flow. A drop in supply pressure is corrected by the flow loop in seconds, long before it could disturb the reaction.

The same structure appears in electric drives (a position loop around a speed loop around a current loop), in hard-disk heads, in the servo motors of robots, and in the open-source autopilots that fly most of the world's hobby and research drones. PX4's multicopter controller has exactly the blocks of \cref{fig:cascade-diagram} — position P, velocity PID, attitude P on quaternions, rate PID — which is why Anemostatos uses them.
\end{curiosity}

\begin{exercises}
\exercise[1] The velocity loop asks for $\vec a_{sp} = (2, 0, 0)\,\si{m/s^2}$ while hovering. Compute $\vec F_{des}$, the tilt, and the collective thrust for $\hat m = \SI{1}{kg}$.
\answer{$\vec F_{des} = (2, 9.81, 0)$\,N (with $\vec g = (0,-9.81,0)$), $|\vec F| = \SI{10.01}{N}$, tilt $\arctan(2/9.81) = 11.5^\circ$.}
\exercise[2] Invert the mixer matrix of \cref{eq:cascade-mixer} symbolically. (Hint: its rows are orthogonal.)
\answer{The rows of $A$ are mutually orthogonal: $AA^\T = \diag(4, 4d^2, 4c_\tau^2, 4d^2)$, so $A^{-1} = A^\T\,\diag(\tfrac14, \tfrac1{4d^2}, \tfrac1{4c_\tau^2}, \tfrac1{4d^2})$, e.g.\ $f_1 = \tfrac T4 + \tfrac{\tau_x}{4d} + \tfrac{\tau_y}{4c_\tau} + \tfrac{\tau_z}{4d}$.}
\exercise[2] Hovering, a pitch torque of \SI{0.2}{N.m} is requested with $d = 0.17/\sqrt2 = \SI{0.12}{m}$. How much must each motor change?
\answer{$\Delta f = \tau_z/(4d) = 0.2/0.48 = \SI{0.42}{N}$: $f_1, f_2$ up, $f_3, f_4$ down by \SI{0.42}{N} each (from \SI{2.45}{N}).}
\exercise[3] Model the closed rate loop as a first-order lag with time constant $\tau_r$, and the attitude loop as $\dot\theta = \omega$, $\omega_{sp} = K_{att}(\theta_{sp} - \theta)$. Find the closed-loop poles of the attitude loop and the condition for $\zeta \ge 0.7$. Evaluate for $K_{att} = 8$ and $\tau_r = \SI{20}{ms}$.
\answer{$\tau_r\ddot\theta + \dot\theta = K_{att}(\theta_{sp} - \theta)$: $\omega_n = \sqrt{K_{att}/\tau_r}$, $\zeta = 1/(2\sqrt{K_{att}\tau_r})$. $\zeta \ge 0.7$ needs $K_{att}\tau_r \le 0.51$. With $8 \cdot 0.02 = 0.16$: $\zeta = 1.25$, well damped.}
\end{exercises}
